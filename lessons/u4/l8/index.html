Choosing a Factoring Strategy

No new technique today! You already know five ways to factor. The new skill is picking the right one for an expression you’ve never seen. We’ll use a short checklist that works every single time.

I can…
  • name the five factoring methods and the clue that tells me to use each one
  • follow the checklist: GCF first, then count the terms, then check every factor
  • spot when taking out a GCF uncovers a difference of squares or an easier trinomial
  • factor an expression completely and check my answer by expanding
AN5 Demonstrate an understanding of common factors and trinomial factoring, concretely, pictorially and symbolically.

The Five Methods, Side by Side

You already know how to run each method. The new skill is picking the right one. Everything from Lessons 1–7 fits in this one table.

MethodTermsWhat to look forPattern
AGCFanyEvery term shares a common factorab+ac=a(b+c)
BDifference of squares2Two perfect squares with a subtraction between thema^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b)
CPerfect square trinomial3First and last terms are perfect squares; middle is 2\times(their roots)a^2\pm2ab+b^2=(a\pm b)^2
DTrinomial, a=13Leading coefficient is 1Two numbers: product c, sum b
ETrinomial, a\neq13Leading coefficient is not 1Two numbers: product ac, sum b (decomposition)

The letters match the warm-up’s tools: A hammer, B pliers, C wrench, D screwdriver, E saw.

Example 1

Name the method

Name the method (or methods, in order) you would use. Do not factor yet.

(a) x^2-4
Two terms, both perfect squares, subtraction: difference of squares
(b) x^2+7x+12
Three terms, a=1, 12 isn’t a square: trinomial by inspection
(c) 4x^2-8x
Both terms share 4x: GCF only
(d) 3x^2+13x+4
Three terms with a=3\neq1: decomposition
(e) x^2+10x+25
25=5^2 and 2(x)(5)=10x: perfect square trinomial
(f) 2x^2-50
GCF first, then the two terms left are a difference of squares.

The Factoring Strategy Checklist

Work these steps in order, every single time:

1GCF firstFactor out the GCF. This is the most commonly missed step.
2Count the terms left2 terms: difference of squares? 3 terms: perfect square first; otherwise inspection (a=1) or decomposition (a\neq1). 4+ terms: grouping (a future lesson).
3Check every factorCan any factor be factored again? Stopping one step early is the other common mistake.

The checklist is often drawn as a flowchart. Pick an expression and step through it: the path lights up one decision at a time, and the written work builds on the right.

The dashed “yes” loop is Step 3: a factor that can still be factored goes back to the top of the chart on its own.

Three terms? Check for a perfect square first

A perfect square trinomial is quick to factor, but only if you notice it. Three questions: is the first term a perfect square? Is the last term? Is the middle term 2\times(the two roots)?

!
The last term of a perfect square trinomial is always positive. (a-b)^2=a^2-2ab+b^2 ends in +b^2. If the last term is negative, skip straight to inspection or decomposition.
3

The Checklist in Action: GCF, Then a Trinomial

Factor 2x^2+20x+42.

1
Every term is divisible by 2, so 2x^2+20x+42=2(x^2+10x+21).
2
Three terms remain. 21 is not a perfect square, so this is not a perfect square trinomial. The leading coefficient is 1, so find two numbers with product 21 and sum 10: 7 and 3. That gives 2(x+7)(x+3).
3
Neither binomial factors again, so we are finished: \boldsymbol{2x^2+20x+42=2(x+7)(x+3)}

Taking out the 2 first turned a decomposition (ac=84) into an easy inspection (product 21).

GCF, then a trinomial

Factor completely.

Step 1: GCF 2: 2(x^2+7x+10)
Step 2: product 10, sum 7: 2 and 5
=\boldsymbol{2(x+2)(x+5)}
Step 3: neither binomial factors further ✓
(b) 4x^2-8x-60
Step 1: GCF 4: 4(x^2-2x-15)
Step 2: product -15, sum -2: -5 and 3
=\boldsymbol{4(x-5)(x+3)}
Step 3: neither binomial factors further ✓
4

Two-Step Factoring: GCF, Then a Difference of Squares

When a two-term expression is not a difference of squares as written, remove the GCF and look again:

4x^2-64=4(x^2-16)=4(x-4)(x+4)

!
Don’t stop one step too early. Leaving the answer as 4(x^2-16) is not finished: x^2-16 is still a difference of squares.

Mixed Practice

With no hint to tell you which method applies, run the checklist from the top: GCF, then count terms, then check the factors. An answer can be true but still not finished. Step 3 catches that:

Is it right? Confirm by expanding

Factoring is expanding run backwards. So expand your answer: if you get the question back, the factoring is right. A quick extra check: put x=1 into both forms.

Example 4

No hints

Factor completely.

(a) x^2-64
No GCF; two terms, both squares:
=\boldsymbol{(x-8)(x+8)}
Check: x^2+8x-8x-64=x^2-64 ✓
(b) x^2-3x-40
No GCF; three terms, a=1.
Product -40, sum -3: -8 and 5
=\boldsymbol{(x-8)(x+5)}
Check: x^2+5x-8x-40=x^2-3x-40 ✓
Example 5

No hints, part 2

Factor completely.

(a) 3x^2+11x+6
No GCF; a=3, so decompose.
ac=18, sum 11: 9 and 2
3x^2+9x+2x+6=3x(x+3)+2(x+3)
=\boldsymbol{(x+3)(3x+2)}
Check: 3x^2+2x+9x+6=3x^2+11x+6 ✓
(b) x^2-12x+36
36=6^2 and 2(x)(6)=12x: a perfect square trinomial.
=\boldsymbol{(x-6)^2}
Check: x^2-6x-6x+36=x^2-12x+36 ✓

Key ideas

Five methods, five clues: GCF (shared factor), difference of squares (2 terms, square − square), perfect square (square, 2\times, square), a=1 (product c, sum b), a\neq1 (product ac, sum b).
Step 1, every time: GCF first. It can uncover a difference of squares (2x^2-50) or make a trinomial easier.
Step 2: count the terms left. 2 → difference of squares. 3 → perfect square first, then inspection or decomposition.
Step 3: check every factor. Can any factor be factored again? Then check by expanding: factoring is expanding run backwards.
