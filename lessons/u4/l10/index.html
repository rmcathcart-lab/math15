Unit Review: Factoring

Every factoring type from the unit in one place, with a worked example for each. Through all of it, one big idea: factoring is expanding run backwards, so every answer can be checked by expanding it again.

I can…
  • take out the greatest common factor, including a negative one
  • factor x^2+bx+c when c is positive and when c is negative
  • spot and factor a difference of squares and a perfect square trinomial, and know that a sum of squares does not factor
  • factor ax^2+bx+c by decomposition
  • choose a strategy, factor completely, and solve equations by factoring

Each type: what it looks like, and its factored form. Tap a card to see an example.

1

Removing the Greatest Common Factor

Find the GCF of the coefficients, times the lowest power of each variable that every term has. Divide every term by it and write GCF × (what’s left). If the leading term is negative, take out a negative GCF.

Example 1

Take out the GCF

Factor completely.

(a) 16x^2-24x
Coefficients: GCF 8; both terms have x.
=\boldsymbol{8x(2x-3)}
Check: 8x(2x-3)=16x^2-24x ✓
(b) -18a^3+27a^2-9a
Leading term negative, so use -9a: =-9a(2a^2-3a+1)
The bracket still factors (ac=2, sum −3: −2, −1):
=\boldsymbol{-9a(2a-1)(a-1)}
2

Trinomials x^2+bx+c, c Positive

When c is positive, the two integers (product c, sum b) have the same sign: both positive if b is positive, both negative if b is negative.

Example 2

Same signs

Factor each trinomial.

(a) x^2+15x+44
Product 44, sum 15: 4 and 11
=\boldsymbol{(x+4)(x+11)}
(b) x^2-12x+35
Product 35, sum −12: -5 and -7
=\boldsymbol{(x-5)(x-7)}
3

Trinomials x^2+bx+c, c Negative

When c is negative, the two integers have opposite signs, and the one with the larger absolute value takes the same sign as b.

!
Right numbers, wrong signs. (x-7)(x+5) has the right numbers for x^2+2x-35 but expands to x^2-2x-35. Expanding catches it every time.
Example 3

Opposite signs

Factor each trinomial.

(a) x^2+2x-35
Product −35, sum 2: 7 and -5
=\boldsymbol{(x+7)(x-5)}
(b) x^2-x-56
Product −56, sum −1: -8 and 7
=\boldsymbol{(x-8)(x+7)}
4

Difference of Squares

Two perfect squares separated by subtraction, A^2-B^2, factor right away as (A+B)(A-B). A sum of squares never factors this way.

Example 4

Square minus square

Factor each binomial.

(a) x^2-121
\sqrt{121}=11
=\boldsymbol{(x-11)(x+11)}
(b) 49x^2-25
Roots 7x and 5
=\boldsymbol{(7x-5)(7x+5)}
5

Perfect Square Trinomials

The first and last terms are perfect squares, and the middle term is twice the product of their roots: A^2+2AB+B^2=(A+B)^2 and A^2-2AB+B^2=(A-B)^2.

Example 5

Square ends, middle 2AB

Factor each perfect square trinomial.

(a) x^2+28x+196
\sqrt{196}=14 and 2(x)(14)=28x ✓
=\boldsymbol{(x+14)^2}
(b) 4x^2-20x+25
Roots 2x and 5; 2(2x)(5)=20x ✓
=\boldsymbol{(2x-5)^2}
6

Factoring ax^2+bx+c by Decomposition

When a\neq1: multiply a\times c, find two integers with that product that add to b, split the middle term with them, then factor by grouping.

Example 6

Product ac, sum b

Factor by decomposition.

(a) 2x^2+13x+6
ac=12, sum 13: 12 and 1
2x^2+12x+x+6
=2x(x+6)+1(x+6)
=\boldsymbol{(x+6)(2x+1)}
(b) 3x^2-x-10
ac=-30, sum −1: -6 and 5
3x^2-6x+5x-10
=3x(x-2)+5(x-2)
=\boldsymbol{(x-2)(3x+5)}
7

Choosing a Strategy

1Always remove a GCF first.
2Count the terms left. Two terms: difference of squares? Three terms: perfect square? If not, sum–product (a=1) or decomposition (a\neq1).
3Check every factor for more factoring, and verify by expanding.
Example 7

Factor completely

Factor each expression completely.

(a) 5x^2-80
GCF 5: 5(x^2-16)
Difference of squares:
=\boldsymbol{5(x-4)(x+4)}
(b) 2x^2+16x+32
GCF 2: 2(x^2+8x+16)
Perfect square trinomial:
=\boldsymbol{2(x+4)^2}
(c) 4x^3-4x^2-24x
GCF 4x: 4x(x^2-x-6)
-3 and 2:
=\boldsymbol{4x(x-3)(x+2)}
8

Solving Equations by Factoring

Move everything to one side so the equation equals 0, factor, then set each factor equal to 0 and solve (the zero product property).

Example 8

Factor, then split

Solve each equation by factoring.

(a) x^2+x-42=0
Product −42, sum 1: 7 and -6
(x+7)(x-6)=0
\boldsymbol{x=-7} or \boldsymbol{x=6}
(b) 3x^2+5x-2=0
ac=-6, sum 5: 6 and -1
3x^2+6x-x-2=3x(x+2)-1(x+2)
(x+2)(3x-1)=0
\boldsymbol{x=-2} or \boldsymbol{x=\tfrac13}

Mixed Review: Putting It All Together

Each question below combines more than one skill from the unit: exactly the kind of question you might see on the unit test. (The checklist explainer in Section 7 replays Challenge 1, and the solving explainer in Section 8 replays Challenge 2.)

Challenges 1–2

Two skills at once

Challenge 1. Factor completely: 6x^3+3x^2-45x
GCF 3x: 3x(2x^2+x-15)
ac=-30, sum 1: 6 and -5
2x^2+6x-5x-15=2x(x+3)-5(x+3)=(x+3)(2x-5)
=\boldsymbol{3x(x+3)(2x-5)}
Challenge 2. Solve: 3x^2=12x
Move everything to one side (do not divide by x, or the root x=0 is lost):
3x^2-12x=0\Rightarrow 3x(x-4)=0
\boldsymbol{x=0} or \boldsymbol{x=4}
Challenge 3

The garden

A rectangular garden has area (x^2+3x-54) m². One side length of the garden is (x+9) m.

(a) Determine a simplified expression for the other side length.
Product −54, sum 3: 9 and -6
x^2+3x-54=(x+9)(x-6)
Since one side is (x+9), the other is \boldsymbol{(x-6)} m.
(b) Determine the actual dimensions of the garden if x=8.
8+9=17 m by 8-6=2 m: 17 m by 2 m
Check: 17\times2=34, and 64+24-54=34 ✓
✓

Key ideas

Factoring is expanding backwards. Every factorization can be checked by expanding it. Always take out the GCF first (a negative one if the lead is negative).
Two terms: A^2-B^2=(A+B)(A-B). A sum of squares A^2+B^2 never factors.
Three terms: perfect square (A\pm B)^2 if the middle is 2AB; else product c, sum b (a=1) or decomposition with product ac (a\neq1).
Solving: make one side 0, factor, set each factor equal to 0. Never divide by x. Then check every factor once more: is the factoring complete?
