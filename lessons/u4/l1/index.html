Multiplying and Factoring: The Reverse Relationship

You already know how to expand: 4(x+3)=4x+12. Factoring is expanding run backwards: start with 4x+12 and find the factors that made it. Today we read multiplying facts backwards, find greatest common factors, and check every factoring by expanding it again.

I can…
  • explain how expanding and factoring are the same fact read in opposite directions
  • find the GCF of two numbers using prime factorization
  • find the GCF of monomials (the GCF of the numbers, and each common letter to its lowest power)
  • check a factoring by expanding it, and fix it if it doesn’t match
  • factor a simple expression by removing the GCF: find, divide, write, verify
AN5 Demonstrate an understanding of common factors and trinomial factoring, concretely, pictorially and symbolically.
Warm-up

How Many Rectangles?

Use all the square tiles, no gaps, to build a rectangle. Turning it doesn’t make a new one. A square counts!

1
Find every rectangle you can build with 12 tiles. How many are there?
3 rectangles: 1\times12, 2\times6, 3\times4. Rows of 5 leave 2 over. Each rectangle is a factor pair of 12.
2
Now do the same with 18 tiles.
3 rectangles: 1\times18, 2\times9, 3\times6.
3
Stand a 12-tile rectangle next to an 18-tile one, the same height. What is the tallest they could be?
Heights that work for both: 1, 2, 3, 6. The tallest is 6 (6\times2 and 6\times3). 6 is the GCF of 12 and 18.
!
The big one. Which pile builds the most different rectangles? And which pile is stuck with one long row? Guess first.
A 36
B 60
C 97
D 100
B, 60 makes the most: 6 rectangles (1\times60, 2\times30, 3\times20, 4\times15, 5\times12, 6\times10). 36 and 100 make 5 each.
C, 97 is stuck: only 1\times97, because 97 is prime. A bigger pile doesn’t mean more rectangles!
+
Challenge your neighbour. What is the smallest pile that builds exactly 4 different rectangles?
24: 1\times24, 2\times12, 3\times8, 4\times6. Nothing smaller makes 4.
Two directions. Given the sides, multiply to get the tiles: 3\times4=12. Given the tiles, find the sides: 12=3\times4. That’s factoring: the same fact, read backwards.
1

What Is Factoring?

Expanding multiplies a factor across a bracket. Factoring runs the same road backwards: it rewrites an expanded expression as a product of factors.

product of factorssum of terms
4(x+3)4x+12
2(x+5)2x+10
x(x+7)x^2+7x
Factoring rewrites an expression as a product of factors: 4x+12=4(x+3). If multiplying your factors back out gives the original exactly, the factoring is correct.

Every multiplying fact is also a factoring fact

Since 2(x+5)=2x+10, we can turn it around: 2x+10=2(x+5). Since x(x+7)=x^2+7x, we know x^2+7x=x(x+7). The common factor can be a number or a letter.

Example 1

From a multiplying fact to a factoring fact

Each multiplication fact is done for you. Use it to write the matching factoring fact.

(a) Since 3(x+4)=3x+12,
then 3x+12=\underline{\qquad}(\underline{\qquad\quad})
3x+12=\boldsymbol{3(x+4)}: just read the multiplication backwards.
(b) Since 8(y-1)=8y-8,
then 8y-8=\underline{\qquad}(\underline{\qquad\quad})
8y-8=\boldsymbol{8(y-1)}
(c) Since x(x+9)=x^2+9x,
then x^2+9x=\underline{\qquad}(\underline{\qquad\quad})
x^2+9x=\boldsymbol{x(x+9)}: the common factor is a letter here.
(d) Since 4x(x-5)=4x^2-20x,
then 4x^2-20x=\underline{\qquad}(\underline{\qquad\quad})
4x^2-20x=\boldsymbol{4x(x-5)}

Recall: The GCF of Numbers

The greatest common factor (GCF) of two numbers is the largest number that divides evenly into both. Find it by prime factorization: break each number into primes, then multiply the primes that appear in every list.
12=\underline{2}\times2\times\underline{3}18=\underline{2}\times\underline{3}\times3
shared: one 2 and one 3 · GCF =2\times3=\boldsymbol{6}

Try it: split each number until only primes are left

Line up the prime factors and look for the shared ones

!
Shared means in every list. 12 has two 2’s but 18 has only one, so only one 2 goes into the GCF.

Shared: one 5. GCF =\boldsymbol{5}
(b) 8,\ 20
8=2\times2\times2
20=2\times2\times5
Shared: two 2’s. GCF =\boldsymbol{4}
(c) 9,\ 24
9=3\times3
24=2\times2\times2\times3
Shared: one 3. GCF =\boldsymbol{3}
(d) 16,\ 28
16=2\times2\times2\times2
28=2\times2\times7
Shared: two 2’s. GCF =\boldsymbol{4}
3

The GCF of a Monomial Set

The same idea works for monomials: just add the letters to the list of factors.

6x^3=2\times\underline{3}\times\underline{x}\times\underline{x}\times x9x^2=\underline{3}\times3\times\underline{x}\times\underline{x}
shared: one 3 and two x’s · GCF =\boldsymbol{3x^2}

The shortcut: by inspection

Shortcut: take the GCF of the coefficients (the numbers), then each common letter raised to its lowest exponent.
GCF of 6x^3 and 9x^2: the GCF of 6 and 9 is 3, and the lowest power of x is x^2, so the GCF is 3x^2.
!
Lowest, not highest. It’s tempting to grab the biggest power, but x^3 doesn’t fit inside x^2. The GCF can only use what every term has.

Verifying a Factoring Is Correct

To check a factored form, expand it back out. If the product gives the original expression exactly, the factoring is correct.

3x+9\overset{?}{=}3(x+3)3(x+3)=3x+9 ✓ correct
4x+8\overset{?}{=}4(x+3)4(x+3)=4x+12 ✗ not 4x + 8The GCF of 4x and 8 is 4, and 8\div4=2, so the correct form is 4(x+2).
!
The front factor multiplies every term. 4(x+3) is 4x+12, not 4x+3.
?
Another quick check: try a number. Put x=2 into both forms. 4x+8=16 but 4(x+3)=20: different, so it’s wrong. (Same answer doesn’t prove it right; expanding does.)
Example 4

Check by expanding

Expand each proposed factored form to check it. If it does not match, write the correct form.

(a) Is 4x+12=4(x+3) correct?
4(x+3)=4x+12: matches the original. ✓ Correct.
(b) Is 5x+15=5(x+2) correct?
5(x+2)=5x+10\neq5x+15. Incorrect.
15\div5=3, so the correct form is \boldsymbol{5(x+3)}.
(c) Is 3x^2+6x=3x(x+2) correct?
3x(x+2)=3x^2+6x: matches. ✓ Correct.
(d) Is 8x+10=2(4x+6) correct?
2(4x+6)=8x+12\neq8x+10. Incorrect.
10\div2=5, so the correct form is \boldsymbol{2(4x+5)}.

Putting It Together: Factor, Then Verify

Think of an expression as the area of a rectangle. Factoring it means finding the rectangle’s side lengths. With algebra tiles: arrange the tiles into equal rows. The tallest side you can make is the GCF.

Without tiles: the four-step routine

1Find the GCF6
2Divide each term6x\div6=x,\ \ 18\div6=3
3Write GCF(what’s left)6x+18=6(x+3)
4Verify by expanding6(x+3)=6x+18\ \checkmark
!
Use the greatest common factor. 6x+18=2(3x+9) expands back correctly, but 3x and 9 still share a 3. Take out 6 and you’re done: 6(x+3).
Example 5

Factor, then verify

Factor each expression completely, and verify your answer by expanding it back out.

(a) 4x+8
GCF of 4x and 8 is 4.
4x\div4=x, 8\div4=2
=\boldsymbol{4(x+2)}
Check: 4(x+2)=4x+8 ✓
(b) 9x+12
GCF is 3 (not 9: it must divide 12 too).
=\boldsymbol{3(3x+4)}
Check: 3(3x+4)=9x+12 ✓
(c) 6x^2+8x
Numbers: GCF 2; both terms have an x.
=\boldsymbol{2x(3x+4)}
Check: 2x(3x+4)=6x^2+8x ✓
Think Why can’t the GCF of 9x+12 be 9?
The GCF has to divide every term. 9 divides 9x, but 12\div9 isn’t a whole number. The biggest number that divides both 9 and 12 is 3.
✓

Key ideas

Factoring is expanding run backwards. 4(x+3)=4x+12 read backwards is 4x+12=4(x+3). An expression is a rectangle’s area; the factors are its sides.
GCF of numbers: write each as a product of primes; multiply the primes found in every list. 12=2\cdot2\cdot3, 18=2\cdot3\cdot3: GCF =6.
GCF of monomials: the GCF of the numbers, times each common letter to its lowest power. GCF of 6x^3 and 9x^2 is 3x^2.
Find, divide, write, verify. Always check by expanding: if you don’t get the original back exactly, fix the factoring.
