Common Factors: Finding and Removing the GCF

Last lesson: factoring is expanding run backwards, and the GCF is the biggest thing every term shares. Today we remove the GCF from longer expressions: two terms, three terms, terms with letters, and expressions that start with a negative. Every answer gets checked by expanding.

I can…
  • factor a binomial or a trinomial by removing the GCF: find it, divide every term, write GCF(what’s left)
  • include a letter in the GCF when every term has it, at its lowest power
  • keep the 1 when a term equals the GCF
  • take out a negative GCF when the first term is negative, so the bracket starts positive
  • check every factoring by expanding it back out
AN5 Demonstrate an understanding of common factors and trinomial factoring, concretely, pictorially and symbolically.
Warm-up

The Party Bag Puzzle

You have 24 candies and 36 stickers. Pack all of it into identical bags, with nothing left over.

1
Can you make 6 identical bags? What goes in each one?
Yes: 24\div6=4 and 36\div6=6, so each bag gets 4 candies + 6 stickers.
2
Why won’t 8 bags work?
The candies split (24\div8=3) but the stickers don’t: 8\times4=32 leaves 4 stickers over. 8 is a factor of 24 but not of 36.
3
What’s the most bags you can make? What goes in each one?
12 bags, each with 2 candies + 3 stickers. The bag counts that work are the common factors 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12. The biggest, 12, is the GCF of 24 and 36.
!
The big one. A friend shows up with 25 balloons. Every candy, sticker and balloon must still go in identical bags. Now what’s the most bags? Guess first.
A 12
B 5
C 25
D 1
D, just 1 bag! 25=5\times5 shares no factor with 24 except 1, so no bag count bigger than 1 splits all three piles. One awkward pile wrecks the whole party.
+
With a neighbour. Swap the 25 balloons for a number that keeps it at 12 bags. What’s the smallest one that works? The biggest under 100?
It must be a multiple of 12. Smallest: 12 (1 per bag). Biggest under 100: 96 (8 per bag).
Let c = a candy and s = a sticker. The whole pile is 24c+36s=12(2c+3s). The GCF is the number of bags; the bracket is one bag. Removing the GCF is packing party bags!
1

Recall the GCF, Then Remove It

The GCF of a set of monomials is the GCF of the numbers times each common letter at its lowest exponent. The GCF of 12x^2 and 18x is 6x.
1Find the GCF
2Divide every term by it
3Write GCF(what’s left)
4Check by expanding

Example: 10x+15=5(2x+3), because 5(2x+3)=10x+15 ✓

Example 1

Remove the GCF from a binomial

Factor each binomial by removing the GCF. Verify by expanding.

(a) 6x+8
GCF =2
=\boldsymbol{2(3x+4)}
Check: 6x+8 ✓
(b) 12y-9
GCF =3
=\boldsymbol{3(4y-3)}
Check: 12y-9 ✓
(c) 10a+25
GCF =5
=\boldsymbol{5(2a+5)}
Check: 10a+25 ✓
(d) 14b-21
GCF =7
=\boldsymbol{7(2b-3)}
Check: 14b-21 ✓
2

Factoring a Trinomial

The same routine works on three terms. There is just one more term to divide in Step 2. For 6x^2+12x+18, the GCF is 6, and dividing each term gives 6(x^2+2x+3).

!
Don’t drop the 1. When a term equals the GCF, dividing leaves a 1: 3x^2+6x+3=3(x^2+2x+\boldsymbol{1}), not 3(x^2+2x). Expanding would catch it: 3(x^2+2x) is missing the +3.
Example 2

Remove the GCF from a trinomial

Factor each trinomial by removing the GCF. Verify by expanding.

(a) 3x^2+6x+9
GCF =3 (no x: the last term has none)
=\boldsymbol{3(x^2+2x+3)}
Check: 3x^2+6x+9 ✓
(b) 10a^2-15a-25
GCF =5
=\boldsymbol{5(2a^2-3a-5)}
Check: 10a^2-15a-25 ✓
(c) 8y^2+12y-4
GCF =4
=\boldsymbol{4(2y^2+3y-1)}
(-4\div4=-1: keep the 1.)
3

A GCF That Includes a Variable

If every term shares a letter, that letter joins the GCF at its lowest exponent. For 8x^3+12x^2: the GCF of 8 and 12 is 4, and the lowest power of x is x^2. So the GCF is 4x^2, and 8x^3+12x^2=4x^2(2x+3).

Dividing by a GCF with letters: cancel what matches

To divide a term by the GCF, write both as factors and cancel the matching ones. Whatever is left on top goes in the bracket.

!
Lowest power, not highest. In 8x^3+12x^2 you can’t take out x^3: the term 12x^2 only has two x’s to give.
Example 3

A GCF with a variable

Factor each polynomial by removing a GCF that includes a variable. Verify by expanding.

(a) 8x^3+12x^2
GCF =4x^2
=\boldsymbol{4x^2(2x+3)}
Check: 8x^3+12x^2 ✓
(b) 12a^2b+18ab^2
Numbers: 6; letters: ab.
=\boldsymbol{6ab(2a+3b)}
Check: 12a^2b+18ab^2 ✓
(c) 4x^3+8x^2+12x
GCF =4x (the lowest power is x^1)
=\boldsymbol{4x(x^2+2x+3)}
Check: 4x^3+8x^2+12x ✓
4

Special Case: A Negative GCF

When the first term is negative, factor out a negative GCF, so that what’s left in the bracket starts with a positive term. For -4x-12, take out -4 (not 4): -4x\div(-4)=x and -12\div(-4)=3, giving -4(x+3).
!
Every sign flips, not just the first. -2x+8=-2(x\boldsymbol{-}4), because 8\div(-2)=-4. Check: -2\cdot(-4)=+8 ✓
Example 4

Remove a negative GCF

Factor each polynomial by removing a negative GCF. Verify by expanding.

(a) -4x-12
GCF =-4
-4x\div(-4)=x, -12\div(-4)=3
=\boldsymbol{-4(x+3)}
(b) -5a+15
GCF =-5
15\div(-5)=-3
=\boldsymbol{-5(a-3)}
(c) -3x^2+9x-6
GCF =-3; every sign flips.
=\boldsymbol{-3(x^2-3x+2)}
Check: -3x^2+9x-6 ✓
5

Putting It Together: Negative GCF with a Variable

A GCF can carry a negative sign and a letter at the same time. For -6x^3-15x^2: the GCF of 6 and 15 is 3, the lowest power of x is x^2, and the first term is negative. So the GCF is -3x^2, and the factored form is -3x^2(2x+5).

Find the error: four common mistakes

Each student’s factoring has one mistake. Expand to find it, then fix it.

✓

Key ideas

Find, divide, write, check. Divide every term by the GCF, write GCF(what’s left), and expand to check: 10x+15=5(2x+3).
Letters in the GCF: only a letter that is in every term, at its lowest power. 8x^3+12x^2=4x^2(2x+3).
Keep the 1. A term that equals the GCF leaves a 1 behind: -8a^3+12a^2-4a=-4a(2a^2-3a+1).
First term negative? Take out a negative GCF. Every sign in the bracket flips, and the bracket starts positive: -4x-12=-4(x+3).
