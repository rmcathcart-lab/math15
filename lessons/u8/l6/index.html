Math 15 · Unit 8 Systems of Linear Equations · Lesson 6

Solving Systems by Elimination, Part 2: Multiplying First

In Lesson 5 a pair of coefficients always matched. Usually they don’t. The fix is one extra step: multiply a whole equation (every term, the constant too) so that a pair finally matches. Then add or subtract exactly as before.

I can…
  • tell when adding or subtracting a system as written eliminates nothing
  • multiply one equation (every term on both sides) when one coefficient is a factor of the other
  • multiply both equations, using the lowest common multiple of the coefficients
  • scan both variables first and choose the easier one to eliminate
  • solve, back-substitute into an original equation, and check the pair in both
RF9 Solve problems that involve systems of linear equations in two variables, graphically and algebraically.
Today: elimination when an equation has to be multiplied first.
Warm-up

Stack the Books

All thick books are the same. All thin books are the same.

1
How tall is the pile of 3 copies of Stack A?
27 cm: three copies of a 9 cm stack, 3\times9=27.
2
The pile and Stack B have the same thick books. Which books stick up above the dashed line?
The pile has 3 thick + 6 thin; Stack B has 3 thick + 1 thin. The thick books line up, so 5 thin books stick up.
3
How thick is one book of each kind?
The 5 thin books make the extra 27-17=10 cm, so a thin book is 10\div5=2 cm. Stack A: thick +\,2+2=9, so a thick book is 5 cm. Check B: 5+5+5+2=17 ✓
!
The big one. Stack P = 2 + 3 , Stack Q = 3 + 2 . How many copies of each make the thin books match? Guess first.
A 2 of P, 3 of Q
B 3 of P, 2 of Q
C It can’t be done
A: 2 of P and 3 of Q. 2 copies of P have 6 thin books; 3 copies of Q have 6 thin books. They match! (B gives 9 thin and 4 thin.) Neither stack alone can be copied to match, so you copy both, each by the other stack’s thin-book count.

Try it with real books: find one notebook’s thickness by measuring only piles.

The books, solved: copy a stack until something matches

Copying a whole stack copies its books and its height. Once one kind of book matches, compare the piles and that kind cancels.

1

When Neither Coefficient Matches

In Lesson 5 one variable always arrived with matching or opposite coefficients. In x+2y=9 and 3x-y=13 the x-coefficients are 1 and 3, and the y-coefficients are 2 and -1. Neither pair matches and neither pair is opposite, so adding or subtracting as written eliminates nothing.

Elimination still works. It just needs one extra step first: multiply an equation by a constant.
2

Multiplying One Equation

Look for a coefficient that is already a factor of the other. Here 1 divides into 2: multiplying Equation 2 by 2 turns -y into -2y, the exact opposite of the 2y above it.

Then solve, back-substitute into an original equation, and check the pair in both.

\begin{array}{rrcr} & \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{x+2y} & = & \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{9}\\ +) & \textcolor{#c27a0e}{6x-2y} & = & \textcolor{#c27a0e}{26}\\[1pt] \hline & \textcolor{#12805a}{7x} & = & \textcolor{#12805a}{35} \end{array}
!
Multiply every term on both sides. The commonest error is scaling the left side and forgetting the constant: 2(3x-y)=2(13) gives 6x-2y=26, not 6x-2y=13.
Example 1

Build the match one step at a time

For the system Eq. 1x+2y=9 Eq. 23x-y=13 build the match one step at a time.

(a) Does adding or subtracting the equations as written eliminate either variable? Explain.
No. The x-coefficients are 1 and 3; the y-coefficients are 2 and -1. Neither pair is equal or opposite.
Adding gives 4x+y=22; subtracting gives -2x+3y=-4. Both still have two variables.
(b) The y-coefficient of Equation 2 is -1. By what constant should you multiply Equation 2 to make it the opposite of 2y? Write the new Equation 2.
Multiply Equation 2 by 2, so -y becomes -2y.
2(3x-y)=2(13)
New Equation 2: \boldsymbol{6x-2y=26} (every term scaled, the 26 too)
(c) Add Equation 1 to your new Equation 2 from part (b). Solve the resulting equation for x.
(x+2y)+(6x-2y)=9+26
7x=35
x=\dfrac{35}{7}=\boldsymbol{5}
(d) Substitute your x into either original equation to find y, then verify in both originals.
Into x+2y=9: 5+2y=9\Rightarrow 2y=4\Rightarrow \boldsymbol{y=2}
Check: 5+2(2)=9 ✓   3(5)-2=13 ✓
Solution: \boldsymbol{(5,\ 2)}
Think Could you eliminate x instead? What would you multiply, and would you add or subtract?
Yes. 1 is a factor of 3, so multiply Equation 1 by 3: 3x+6y=27. Now both have 3x (same sign), so subtract: (3x+6y)-(3x-y)=27-13, 7y=14, y=2. Same answer.
3

Multiplying Both Equations

When neither coefficient divides the other (say 3 and -2), both equations must be multiplied.

Take the lowest common multiple (LCM) of the two coefficients: 3 and 2 give 6. Then scale each equation so that variable becomes +6y in one and -6y in the other. Using the LCM, rather than any common multiple, keeps the numbers as small as possible.
Example 2

Multiply both equations

For the system Eq. 12x+3y=17 Eq. 23x-2y=6 eliminate y by multiplying both equations.

(a) The y-coefficients are 3 and -2. Neither is a factor of the other; find the lowest common multiple of 3 and 2.
Multiples of 3: 3,\ 6,\ 9,\ \ldots Multiples of 2: 2,\ 4,\ 6,\ \ldots
The LCM is 6, so aim for +6y in one equation and -6y in the other.
(b) Multiply Equation 1 by the constant that turns its y-coefficient into your answer to (a), and Equation 2 by the constant giving the opposite of it. Write both new equations.
Equation 1 \times\,2: \boldsymbol{4x+6y=34}
Equation 2 \times\,3: \boldsymbol{9x-6y=18}
The y-terms are now +6y and -6y.
(c) Add your two new equations from part (b). Solve the resulting equation for x.
(4x+6y)+(9x-6y)=34+18
13x=52
x=\dfrac{52}{13}=\boldsymbol{4}
(d) Substitute your x into either original equation to find y, then verify in both originals.
Into 2x+3y=17: 8+3y=17\Rightarrow 3y=9\Rightarrow \boldsymbol{y=3}
Check: 2(4)+3(3)=17 ✓   3(4)-2(3)=6 ✓
Solution: \boldsymbol{(4,\ 3)}
Math HistoryChina · about 2000 years ago

Multiply first, then subtract: with counting rods

The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art is a Chinese book about 2000 years old. One problem: 3 bundles of top-grade grain, 2 of medium and 1 of low yield 39 dou; 2, 3 and 1 bundles yield 34; 1, 2 and 3 yield 26.

Each equation became a column of counting rods. To make the top numbers match, they multiplied a whole column by 3, then subtracted another column until the top number was 0. That is today’s lesson!

With x, y, z the grain per bundle, multiply 2x+3y+z=34 by 3. Then subtract 3x+2y+z=39.
6x+9y+3z=102, then \boldsymbol{3x+7y+2z=63}. Subtract 3x+2y+z=39 once more and the x is gone: 5y+z=24.
4

Choosing the Easier Variable

Scan both variables before you multiply anything. If one variable can be eliminated by multiplying just one equation, that route is faster and far less error-prone than one needing both.

1Choose a variable
2Multiply one or both equations
3Add or subtract
4Solve
5Back-substitute
6State the ordered pair
Example 3

Compare the two routes first

For the system Eq. 15x-2y=14 Eq. 23x+4y=24 compare the two routes before committing.

(a) For each variable, decide whether you would multiply just one equation or both. Which variable is easier to eliminate?
x: 5 and 3 share no factor: multiply both (Eq. 1 by 3, Eq. 2 by 5) to get 15x.
y: -2 and 4; 2 divides 4, so multiply one (Eq. 1 by 2), and the signs are already opposite, so add.
\boldsymbol{y} is easier.
(b) Eliminate the easier variable (multiply, then add or subtract) and solve for x.
Equation 1 \times2: 10x-4y=28
(10x-4y)+(3x+4y)=28+24
13x=52, so \boldsymbol{x=4}
(c) Substitute to find y, then verify the solution in both original equations.
Into 3x+4y=24: 3(4)+4y=24
\Rightarrow 4y=12\Rightarrow \boldsymbol{y=3}
Check: 5(4)-2(3)=14 ✓
3(4)+4(3)=24 ✓
Solution: \boldsymbol{(4,\ 3)}
Example 4

Both equations need multiplying

For the system Eq. 13x+4y=29 Eq. 25x-6y=-15 both equations will need multiplying.

(a) The y-coefficients are 4 and -6. Find their lowest common multiple, and state the constant you would multiply each equation by to reach it.
LCM of 4 and 6 is 12.
Equation 1 \times\,\boldsymbol{3} gives +12y; Equation 2 \times\,\boldsymbol{2} gives -12y.
(b) Multiply both equations as in part (a), then add or subtract to eliminate y. Solve for x.
9x+12y=87 and 10x-12y=-30
Opposites, so add:
19x=57, so x=\dfrac{57}{19}=\boldsymbol{3}
(c) Substitute to find y, then verify the solution in both original equations.
Into 3x+4y=29: 9+4y=29
\Rightarrow 4y=20\Rightarrow \boldsymbol{y=5}
Check: 3(3)+4(5)=29 ✓
5(3)-6(5)=15-30=-15 ✓
Solution: \boldsymbol{(3,\ 5)}
Example 5

The bake sale

A bake sale sells cookies and cupcakes. Friday: 3 cookies and 5 cupcakes sold for $19. Saturday: 4 cookies and 2 cupcakes sold for $16.

(a) Let x be the price of a cookie and y the price of a cupcake, both in dollars. Write a system of two equations describing Friday’s and Saturday’s sales.
Friday: \boldsymbol{3x+5y=19}
Saturday: \boldsymbol{4x+2y=16}
(number of cookies)\times(cookie price) + (number of cupcakes)\times(cupcake price) = the day’s total.
(b) Solve your system by elimination. (Both equations will need to be multiplied.)
Eliminate y: LCM of 5 and 2 is 10. Eq. 1 \times2, Eq. 2 \times5:
6x+10y=38, 20x+10y=80
Same sign, so subtract: -14x=-42, \boldsymbol{x=3}
4(3)+2y=16\Rightarrow 2y=4\Rightarrow \boldsymbol{y=2}
(c) State the price of a cookie and the price of a cupcake, then verify your solution in both original equations.
A cookie costs $3 and a cupcake costs $2.
Check: 3(3)+5(2)=9+10=19 ✓
4(3)+2(2)=12+4=16 ✓
!
Bigger minus smaller. In (b), subtracting the other way, (20x+10y)-(6x+10y)=80-38, gives 14x=42: the same x=3 with no negatives to carry.

Key ideas

Nothing matches? If no pair of coefficients is equal or opposite, adding or subtracting leaves both variables. Multiply first to make a pair match.
Multiply every term. Scaling an equation multiplies both sides, the constant too: 2(3x-y)=2(13) gives 6x-2y=26. Same line, same solutions.
One or both? If one coefficient is a factor of the other, multiply one equation. If not, multiply both to the lowest common multiple: 3 and 2 → 6.
Choose the easier variable, then add (opposite signs) or subtract (same sign), solve, back-substitute into an original equation, and check in both.
