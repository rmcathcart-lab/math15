Solving Word Problems with Systems of Equations

Turning a paragraph about two unknown quantities into two equations, then solving it. The algebra is nothing new: substitution and elimination already work. The new skill is translating the words.

I can…
  • use the four stages: define the two variables, write two equations, solve, and answer in a sentence with a check
  • turn key words into +, − and × (sum, more than, fewer than, twice …)
  • solve number and age problems: a sum and a comparison
  • solve cost and purchase problems: one equation counts the items, the other totals their value
  • solve mixture problems: one equation for the total amount, one for the total value

The Four-Stage Method

The algebra is nothing new: substitution and elimination already work. The new skill is translating. Every problem in this lesson runs the same four stages.

Stage 1
Define the two variables: say exactly what each letter counts, with units.
Stage 2
Write two equations: one from each fact in the problem.
Stage 3
Solve by substitution or elimination.
Stage 4
Answer in a sentence, and check that the answer is reasonable.

Watch the walkthrough travel through the four stages: the unknowns become let statements, each fact becomes a row, and each row becomes an equation.

Walkthrough. “Two numbers have a sum of 22; the larger is 6 more than the smaller.” Let L= larger, S= smaller, so L+S=22 and L=S+6. Substituting, (S+6)+S=22, giving 2S=16, S=8, L=14. The numbers are 14 and 8: 14+8=22 ✓ and 14=8+6 ✓.

Key words name the operation

+sum, total, altogether, and more than
−difference, less than, fewer than
×twice, double, triple, “3 times as many”
!
“More than” and “less than” flip the order. “4 fewer cats than dogs” starts with 4, but the 4 is taken away from the dogs: c=d-4. Always read your equation back with small numbers to test it.

Stage 4: does the answer make sense?

Check the answer in both equations, then check it against the words: counts of people or things must be whole numbers, ages and amounts must be positive, and the sentence must answer the question that was asked.
Example 1

Boys and girls in a classroom

There are 30 students in Ms. Alvarez’s classroom. There are 8 more girls than boys.

(a) Assign a variable to each unknown.
Let b= ________   Let g= ________
Let b= the number of boys.
Let g= the number of girls.
Both letters count people, so both must be whole numbers.
(b) Translate the two given facts into a system.
Equation 1: \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{b+g=30}
Equation 2: \textcolor{#c27a0e}{g=b+8}
“30 students” is the total; “8 more girls” compares g to b.
(c) Solve the system to find the number of boys and the number of girls.
Equation 2 is already solved for g, so substitute it into Equation 1:
b+(\textcolor{#c27a0e}{b+8})=30
2b+8=30\ \Rightarrow\ 2b=22\ \Rightarrow\ \boldsymbol{b=11}
g=11+8=\boldsymbol{19}
(d) State the answer in words, and check both values in the original equations.
There are \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{11} boys and \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{19} girls.
Check: 11+19=30 ✓   19=11+8 ✓

Number and Age Word Problems

Two related numbers (often two people’s ages) are tied together by a sum and by a comparison (“5 years older than”, “3 times as old as”). The comparison is usually already isolated, so substitution is quickest.

The sum
add the two unknowns
J+A=27
The comparison
one unknown, written using the other
J=A+5  or  M=3K

A bar model shows why substitution works: the bigger bar is the smaller bar plus a bit more (or several copies of it).

Example 2

Jordan and Ava

Jordan is 5 years older than his sister Ava. The sum of their ages is 27.

(a) Let J represent Jordan’s age and A represent Ava’s age. Write a system of two equations that models this situation.
Equation 1: \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{J=A+5}    Equation 2: \textcolor{#c27a0e}{J+A=27}
“5 years older than Ava” is the comparison; “the sum of their ages is 27” is the total.
(b) Solve the system using substitution.
Equation 1 is already solved for J, so replace J in Equation 2:
(\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{A+5})+A=27
2A+5=27\ \Rightarrow\ 2A=22\ \Rightarrow\ \boldsymbol{A=11}
J=11+5=\boldsymbol{16}
(c) State both ages in words, and check your answer.
Jordan is \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{16} and Ava is \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{11}.
Check: 16+11=27 ✓   16=11+5 ✓

Cost and Purchase Word Problems

Two different items sell at two different prices. One equation counts how many items there are altogether; the other totals their value: price times quantity for each item, added. Both come out in standard form, so elimination usually fits best.

Count equation
every item counts as 1, whatever it costs
h+j=\text{number of items}
Value equation
each item counts as its price in dollars
3h+2j=\text{total sales}
!
Don’t mix a count with a value. h+j=97 adds hot dogs to dollars. Read the units in each equation: items + items = items; dollars + dollars = dollars.
Example 3

Hot dogs and juice boxes

At the school canteen, hot dogs cost $3 and juice boxes cost $2. On Monday, the canteen sold a combined total of 40 hot dogs and juice boxes, for total sales of $97.

(a) Let h represent the number of hot dogs sold and j represent the number of juice boxes sold. Write a system of two equations (one for the total number of items, one for the total sales).
Count equation: \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{h+j=40}    Value equation: \textcolor{#c27a0e}{3h+2j=97}
The count equation adds items; the value equation adds dollars (price × quantity).
(b) Solve the system using elimination.
Multiply the count equation by 2 so the j terms match:
2h+2j=80
3h+2j=97
Subtract the first from the second: \boldsymbol{h=17}
Then 17+j=40, so \boldsymbol{j=23}
Check: 3(17)+2(23)=51+46=97 ✓
(c) State the answer in words, and check.
The canteen sold \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{17} hot dogs and \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{23} juice boxes, and 17+23=40 items ✓.
Example 4

Concert tickets

At a school concert, student tickets cost $5 and adult tickets cost $8. A total of 50 tickets were sold, for total ticket revenue of $316.

(a) Let s represent the number of student tickets sold and a represent the number of adult tickets sold. Write a system of two equations, then solve it using elimination.
Count: \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{s+a=50}    Value: \textcolor{#c27a0e}{5s+8a=316}
Multiply the count equation by 5: 5s+5a=250
Subtract it from the value equation: 3a=66\ \Rightarrow\ \boldsymbol{a=22}
Then s+22=50, so \boldsymbol{s=28}
Check: 5(28)+8(22)=140+176=316 ✓
(b) State the answer in words, and check.
\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{28} student tickets and \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{22} adult tickets were sold (28+22=50 tickets ✓).

Examples 3 and 4 check: the four stages

Mixture Word Problems

A mixture blends two ingredients with different prices per kilogram. The same two equations return: one for the total amount mixed, one for the total value. Define each variable as a number of kilograms so the units in the value equation agree.

Amount (kg)× Price ($/kg)= Value ($)

The Amount column adds up: that is Eq. 1.
The Value column adds up: that is Eq. 2.
The prices never add up.
Example 5

A party mix

A snack shop mixes cashews, which cost $8/kg, with peanuts, which cost $3/kg, to make a party mix. The mix weighs 20 kg in total and sells for $110.

(a) Let c represent the number of kg of cashews used and p represent the number of kg of peanuts used. Write a system of two equations (one for the total weight, one for the total value), then solve it using substitution.
Weight: \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{c+p=20}    Value: \textcolor{#c27a0e}{8c+3p=110}
Isolate p in the weight equation: p=20-c
8c+3(20-c)=110
8c+60-3c=110\ \Rightarrow\ 5c=50\ \Rightarrow\ \boldsymbol{c=10}
p=20-10=\boldsymbol{10}
Check: 8(10)+3(10)=80+30=110 dollars ✓
(b) State how many kg of each ingredient are in the mix, and check your answer.
The mix contains \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{10} kg of cashews and \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{10} kg of peanuts.
Check: 10+10=20 kg ✓ and 80+30=110 dollars ✓

Key ideas

Four stages. 1 Define the two variables (what each letter counts). 2 Two equations, one from each fact. 3 Solve by substitution or elimination. 4 Answer in a sentence and check.
Key words. Sum, total, more than \to +. Difference, less than, fewer than \to -. Twice, triple \to \times. “4 fewer cats than dogs” is c=d-4: read it back to test it.
Count and value. One equation counts items (h+j=40), the other totals dollars (3h+2j=97). Mixtures: total kilograms and total value. Elimination or substitution both work.
Check against the story. Both equations come true, counts are whole numbers, ages and amounts are positive, and the sentence answers the question that was asked.
