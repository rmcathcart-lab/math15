Solving Systems by Elimination, Part 1: When Coefficients Already Match

Substitution always works, but sometimes it drags fractions along. Elimination is a shortcut: line the two equations up and add or subtract them, so one variable vanishes. What is left is one equation with one variable, the kind you already know how to solve.

I can…
  • spot a variable whose coefficients match (same number, same sign) or are opposites (same number, opposite signs)
  • subtract the equations when the coefficients match, lining up columns and changing the sign of every term I subtract, the constant too
  • add the equations when the coefficients are opposites, because a number plus its opposite is 0
  • solve the one-variable equation, back-substitute into either original equation, and check in both

Stack the two receipts and combine them, item by item and total by total. Something cancels and leaves just one kind of item. That move has a name: elimination.

1

The Idea Behind Elimination

Substitution always works, but isolating a variable can create fractions to drag through every step. Elimination lines the two equations up and combines them so one variable disappears entirely.

If 5x+2y=16 and 5x-3y=1, subtracting gives
(5x+2y)-(5x-3y)=16-1
so 5y=15 and y=3. The x-terms cancelled because 5x-5x=0, leaving one equation in one variable.

Think of each equation as a level balance: the left side and the right side weigh the same. Put both balances on one big balance and it is still level.

Eliminating by Subtraction (Matching Coefficients)

If a variable has the exact same coefficient in both equations (same number, same sign), subtract the equations. Line up the x-terms, the y-terms and the constants in columns, and subtract each column.

Then solve the one-variable equation and back-substitute into either original equation.

\begin{array}{rrcr} & \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{3x+4y} & = & \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{23}\\ -) & \textcolor{#c27a0e}{3x-2y} & = & \textcolor{#c27a0e}{11}\\[1pt] \hline & \textcolor{#12805a}{6y} & = & \textcolor{#12805a}{12} \end{array}
!
Subtracting changes the sign of every term of the equation you subtract, the constant included. Here 4y-(-2y)=4y+2y=6y, and the constants give 23-11, not 23+11. Writing brackets, (3x+4y)-(3x-2y), keeps the signs straight.
Example 1

Subtract, one step at a time

For the system Eq. 13x+4y=23 Eq. 23x-2y=11 work through the elimination one step at a time.

(a) Do the x-terms have the same coefficient? Which variable does subtracting Eq. 2 from Eq. 1 remove?
Yes: both equations start with 3x (same number, same sign).
Since 3x-3x=0, subtracting removes \boldsymbol{x}.
(b) Subtract Equation 2 from Equation 1, lining up like terms. Write the resulting one-variable equation.
(3x+4y)-(3x-2y)=23-11
3x-3x+4y+2y=12 (the -2y becomes +2y)
\boldsymbol{6y=12}
(c) Solve the equation from part (b) for y.
6y=12
y=\dfrac{12}{6}, so \boldsymbol{y=2}
(d) Substitute your y into either original equation to find x, then check in both originals.
3x+4(2)=23\ \Rightarrow\ 3x=15\ \Rightarrow\ \boldsymbol{x=5}
Check: 3(5)+4(2)=23 ✓   3(5)-2(2)=11 ✓
The solution is \boldsymbol{(5,\ 2)}.
Think What if you subtract the other way, Eq. 2 - Eq. 1? Do you get a different answer?
Same answer. (3x-2y)-(3x+4y)=11-23 gives -6y=-12, so y=2 again. Either order works; pick the one that keeps the numbers positive.

Back-substitute into either original equation

Once you know one variable, put it into either original equation to find the other. Both give the same answer, so pick the one with the friendlier numbers. Then check in both.

3

Eliminating by Addition (Opposite Coefficients)

Sometimes the coefficients are not equal but opposite: same number, opposite signs, like +5y and -5y. Then add the two equations: left sides together, right sides together.

A number plus its opposite is 0, so 5y+(-5y)=0 and the variable is gone. Solve, then back-substitute and check in both original equations, exactly as before.
Example 2

Opposites: add the equations

For the system Eq. 12x+5y=23 Eq. 26x-5y=9 eliminate by adding.

(a) Are the y-terms opposites? Which variable does adding the two equations remove?
Yes: +5y and -5y are the same number with opposite signs.
Since 5y+(-5y)=0, adding removes \boldsymbol{y}.
(b) Add the two equations. Write the resulting one-variable equation, then solve it for x.
(2x+5y)+(6x-5y)=23+9
8x+0y=32
8x=32, so \boldsymbol{x=4}
(c) Substitute your x into either original equation to find y, then check both originals.
2(4)+5y=23\ \Rightarrow\ 5y=15\ \Rightarrow\ \boldsymbol{y=3}
Check: 2(4)+5(3)=23 ✓
6(4)-5(3)=9 ✓
The solution is \boldsymbol{(4,\ 3)}.

What elimination does on the graph

Back in Lesson 1, the solution was the point where the two lines cross. Adding or subtracting the equations makes a new line that still goes through that crossing. Elimination picks the new line that is flat (y=\ldots) or straight up and down (x=\ldots), so you can read one coordinate right off it.

4

Deciding: Add or Subtract?

Before touching a new system, ask one question: do a variable’s coefficients match, or are they opposite?

Same number, same sign → subtract
+4x and +4x
4x-4x=0
Same number, opposite signs → add
+4x and -4x
4x+(-4x)=0

Either way one variable disappears, leaving an equation you already know how to solve.

Example 3

Decide, eliminate, solve

For the system Eq. 14x+3y=18 Eq. 27x+3y=36 decide, eliminate, and solve.

(a) Add or subtract? Look at the coefficients of y in both equations and decide.
The y-coefficients are +3 and +3: same number, same sign.
Matching coefficients \Rightarrow subtract the equations.
(b) Eliminate y and solve the resulting equation for x.
(7x+3y)-(4x+3y)=36-18
3x=18, so \boldsymbol{x=6}
(c) Back-substitute to find y, then check both original equations.
4(6)+3y=18\ \Rightarrow\ 3y=-6\ \Rightarrow\ \boldsymbol{y=-2}
Check: 4(6)+3(-2)=18 ✓
7(6)+3(-2)=36 ✓
The solution is \boldsymbol{(6,\ -2)}.
Think The answer in (b) subtracts Eq. 1 from Eq. 2, not Eq. 2 from Eq. 1. Why is that order friendlier here?
Bigger minus smaller keeps the numbers positive: 7x-4x=3x and 36-18=18. The other order gives -3x=-18: the same x=6, just more negatives to handle.
Example 4

Decide, eliminate, solve

For the system Eq. 14x+y=-7 Eq. 2-4x+3y=27 decide, eliminate, and solve.

(a) Add or subtract? Look at the coefficients of x in both equations and decide.
The x-coefficients are +4 and -4: same number, opposite signs.
Opposite coefficients \Rightarrow add the equations.
(b) Eliminate x and solve the resulting equation for y.
(4x+y)+(-4x+3y)=-7+27
4y=20, so \boldsymbol{y=5}
(c) Back-substitute to find x, then check both original equations.
4x+5=-7\ \Rightarrow\ 4x=-12\ \Rightarrow\ \boldsymbol{x=-3}
Check: 4(-3)+5=-7 ✓
-4(-3)+3(5)=27 ✓
The solution is \boldsymbol{(-3,\ 5)}.
Example 5

Both negative: watch your signs

For Eq. 1-2x+5y=-34 Eq. 2-2x-3y=-2 both coefficients of x are negative. Watch your signs.

(a) Add or subtract? Look at the coefficients of x in both equations and decide.
Both x-coefficients are -2: same number and the same (negative) sign, so they match; they are not opposites.
Subtract the equations.
(b) Eliminate x and solve the resulting equation for y.
(-2x+5y)-(-2x-3y)=-34-(-2)
-2x+2x+5y+3y=-32
8y=-32, so \boldsymbol{y=-4}
(c) Back-substitute to find x, then check both original equations.
-2x+5(-4)=-34
\Rightarrow\ -2x=-14\ \Rightarrow\ \boldsymbol{x=7}
Check: -2(7)+5(-4)=-34 ✓
-2(7)-3(-4)=-2 ✓
The solution is \boldsymbol{(7,\ -4)}.
!
Two negatives are the same sign. -2x and -2x match, so subtract: -2x-(-2x)=-2x+2x=0. Adding them would give -4x, and nothing vanishes. And the constants: -34-(-2)=-34+2=-32.

Key ideas

The idea. Line the equations up and combine them, left side with left side and right side with right side. Choose the combination so one variable cancels, leaving one equation in one variable.
Same sign → subtract. +3x and +3x, or -2x and -2x, match. Subtract in columns and change the sign of every term you subtract, the constant too: (\;\cdots)-(\;\cdots).
Opposite signs → add. +5y and -5y are opposites: 5y+(-5y)=0, a zero pair. Add the left sides and add the right sides.
Finish the job. Solve for the variable that is left, back-substitute into either original equation, and check the pair in both equations.
