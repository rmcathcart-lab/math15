Solving Systems by Substitution, Part 1: When a Variable Is Already Isolated

Trading a variable for an equal expression gets an exact solution without graphing. If one equation says y=2x+1, then y and 2x+1 are two names for the same value. Swap one for the other in the other equation, and two equations with two unknowns become one equation with one unknown.

I can…
  • explain why I can substitute an expression for a variable it equals
  • solve a system by substitution when one equation is already solved for y or for x, using the five steps
  • put the expression in brackets and multiply every term inside, even when there is a minus sign in front
  • write the solution as an ordered pair (x,\ y) and check it in both original equations

Warm-up

Swap It Out!

Every scale is perfectly balanced. Things that balance weigh the same.

1
Scale A: how many balls balance 2 boxes?
6 balls. Each box balances 3 balls, so 2 boxes balance 3+3=6.
2
Scale B: swap the box for 3 balls. How many balls balance 25 g?
5 balls. The box and 3 balls weigh the same, so the scale stays balanced: 3+2=5 balls balance 25 g.
3
How heavy is one ball? One box?
Ball =25\div5= 5 g. Box =3\times5= 15 g. Check Scale B: 15+5+5=25 g ✓
!
The big one. A new shop: a tiny gold nugget, cans and marbles. It takes two swaps! How heavy is the tiny nugget? Guess first.
A 10 g
B 33 g
C 60 g
C: the nugget weighs 60 g. Swap 1 (Scale C): the nugget becomes 2 cans. Swap 2 (Scale D): every can becomes 3 marbles. Scale E is now 6+3+1=10 marbles =100 g, so a marble is 10 g, a can is 3\times10= 30 g and the nugget is 2\times30= 60 g. Check: 60+30+10=100 g ✓
The nugget is drawn tiny, so A is tempting; B splits 100 three ways. The smallest thing on the pan is more than half the weight!

1

The Idea Behind Substitution

Graphing gives a solution only as accurate as the grid; substitution gives the exact answer every time. If one equation says y=2x+1, then “y” and “2x+1” are two names for the same value, so wherever y appears in the other equation we may write 2x+1 instead. That other equation then holds only x: one equation, one unknown, which we already know how to solve.

Substitute means “swap in something equal.” On a balanced scale you can swap a box for the 3 balls it balances. In an equation you can swap y for the expression it equals.
2

The Substitution Process, Step by Step

Step 1
Find
the equation already written as y=\ldots or x=\ldots
Step 2
Substitute
that expression, in brackets, into the other equation
Step 3
Solve
the new equation. It has only one variable.
Step 4
Back-substitute
that value into the isolated equation to get the other variable
Step 5
State & check
the ordered pair (x,\ y), in both original equations

Substitute into the other equation

Putting the expression back into the equation it came from collapses to a true-but-useless statement such as 2x+1=2x+1.

Think What happens if you swap 2x+1 for y in y=2x+1 itself? Try it.
You get 2x+1=2x+1. It is true for every x, so it tells you nothing. The expression came from that equation. Always use the other one.
Example 1

One step at a time

Solve \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{y=2x+1} and \textcolor{#c27a0e}{3x+y=16} by substitution, one step at a time.

Step 1: Which equation already has a variable isolated? Write it down.
y=2x+1 is already solved for y. So substitute into the other equation, 3x+y=16.
Step 2: Substitute 2x+1 in place of y in 3x+y=16. The new equation should contain only x.
3x+y=16
3x+(2x+1)=16
\boldsymbol{5x+1=16}: only x left.
Step 3: Solve the equation from Step 2 for x.
5x+1=16
5x=15
\boldsymbol{x=3}
Step 4: Substitute that x-value back into y=2x+1 to find y.
y=2(3)+1
y=6+1
\boldsymbol{y=7}
Step 5: Write the solution as an ordered pair (x,\ y), then check it in both original equations.
Solution: \boldsymbol{(3,\ 7)}
Check in y=2x+1: 2(3)+1=7 ✓    Check in 3x+y=16: 3(3)+7=9+7=16 ✓
It works in both, so (3,\ 7) solves the system.
3

Practicing Substitution

Each system below already has one equation solved for a variable. Substitute, solve, back-substitute, and check. The isolated variable may be x rather than y, and a solution may come out negative. Both are perfectly normal.

Brackets go in with the expression. The number in front of the variable multiplies the whole expression: 3y with y=x-4 becomes 3(x-4)=3x-12, not 3x-4.
!
A minus in front of the bracket changes both terms. -(-2x+5)=+2x-5, and -2(3x-7)=-6x+14.
Example 2

The number in front multiplies the bracket

Solve \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{y=x-4} and \textcolor{#c27a0e}{2x+3y=18} by substitution.

(a) Substitute x-4 for y in the second equation and solve for x.
2x+3y=18
2x+3(x-4)=18
2x+3x-12=18
5x-12=18
5x=30
\boldsymbol{x=6}
(b) Substitute your x-value back into y=x-4 to find y.
y=x-4
y=6-4
\boldsymbol{y=2}
Solution: \boldsymbol{(6,\ 2)}
(c) Check your solution in both original equations.
y=x-4: 2=6-4 ✓
2x+3y=18:
2(6)+3(2)=12+6=18 ✓
Example 3

This time x is isolated

Solve \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{x=3y+1} and \textcolor{#c27a0e}{x+2y=16} by substitution.

(a) Substitute the expression for x into the second equation and solve the system.
x+2y=16
(3y+1)+2y=16
5y+1=16\ \Rightarrow\ 5y=15\ \Rightarrow\ \boldsymbol{y=3}
x=3(3)+1=\boldsymbol{10}
Solution: \boldsymbol{(10,\ 3)}
(b) Check your solution in both original equations.
x=3y+1: 3(3)+1=10 ✓
x+2y=16: 10+2(3)=10+6=16 ✓

Which value do you find first?

You always find the other variable first: when y is isolated, Step 3 gives x; when x is isolated, Step 3 gives y. Either way, the answer is written (x,\ y): x first.

Example 4

A minus sign in front, and a negative answer

Solve \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{y=-2x+5} and \textcolor{#c27a0e}{3x-y=10} by substitution, and check. One variable will come out negative.

Solve and check.
Substitute -2x+5 for y in 3x-y=10. Watch the subtraction sign: it changes both terms.
3x-(-2x+5)=10\ \Rightarrow\ 3x+2x-5=10\ \Rightarrow\ 5x-5=10\ \Rightarrow\ 5x=15\ \Rightarrow\ \boldsymbol{x=3}
Back-substitute: y=-2(3)+5=-6+5=\boldsymbol{-1}.   Solution: \boldsymbol{(3,\ -1)}
Check: -2(3)+5=-1 ✓   3(3)-(-1)=9+1=10 ✓
4

Checking the Solution

Check every ordered pair in both original equations (not rearranged ones), and confirm each left side equals its right side. A check that does not balance catches sign errors and missed steps, and a pair that satisfies only one equation is not a solution to the system at all.

Example 5

Show the substitution, then check each equation on its own line

Solve \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{y=3x-7} and \textcolor{#c27a0e}{5x-2y=11} by substitution. Show the substitution step clearly, then check the solution in both original equations, writing each check as its own line of work.

Solve and check.
Substitution step: replace y with 3x-7 in 5x-2y=11.
5x-2(3x-7)=11\ \Rightarrow\ 5x-6x+14=11\ \Rightarrow\ -x+14=11\ \Rightarrow\ -x=-3\ \Rightarrow\ \boldsymbol{x=3}
y=3(3)-7=9-7=\boldsymbol{2}.   Solution: \boldsymbol{(3,\ 2)}
Check 1 (y=3x-7): 2=3(3)-7=2 ✓
Check 2 (5x-2y=11): 5(3)-2(2)=15-4=11 ✓

Exact, not estimated

Remember Section 1: a graph is only as accurate as its grid. When the lines cross between grid lines, reading the graph gives a guess, and a guess usually fails the check. Substitution gives the exact crossing point.

✓

Key ideas

Two names, one value. If y=2x+1, then wherever y appears in the other equation you may write (2x+1). Two unknowns become one.
The five steps. Find the isolated equation; substitute into the other equation; solve; back-substitute; state (x,\ y) and check.
Brackets go in with it. The number in front multiplies every term: 3(x-4)=3x-12, -(-2x+5)=2x-5, -2(3x-7)=-6x+14.
Check in BOTH originals. A pair that works in only one equation is not the solution. Write x first in the pair, even when you found y first.
