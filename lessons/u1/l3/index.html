Prime Factorization, GCF, and LCM

Every whole number bigger than 1 is built by multiplying primes. Today we break numbers into their prime building blocks, then use those primes to find the greatest common factor and the lowest common multiple, and decide which one a word problem needs.

I can…
  • tell whether a whole number is prime or composite
  • find a prime factorization with a division ladder or a factor tree, and write it with exponents
  • find the GCF of a group of numbers: shared primes, lowest power
  • find the LCM of a group of numbers: every prime, highest power
  • decide whether a word problem needs the GCF or the LCM, and solve it

Prime and Composite Numbers

Prime number: a whole number greater than 1 whose only factors are 1 and itself.
7 is prime: only 1 × 7.
Composite number: has more than two factors.
12 has 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12.
1 is neither prime nor composite: it has only one factor.

Math Historyc. 300 BCE · Euclid’s Elements, Book IX

Euclid’s proof: the primes never run out

Around 300 BCE, the Greek mathematician Euclid wrote the Elements, a math book that was still used in schools 2 000 years later. In it he proved there is no largest prime.

His idea: take any list of primes, multiply them and add 1. None of the primes on the list divides the new number (each leaves a remainder of 1). So the new number is a new prime, or its prime factors are new primes.

2 × 3 × 5 × 7 × 11 × 13 + 1 = 30 031. Is 30 031 prime? (Hint: try 59.)
30 031 ÷ 59 = 509, so it is composite: 30 031 = 59 × 509. And 509 is prime. So 59 and 509 are new primes, just as Euclid said.

Prime Factorization

A prime factorization writes a number as a product of primes, usually in exponent form. Two organized methods give the same result.
Division ladder
Divide repeatedly by the smallest prime that fits. Stop at 1. The primes are down the left side.
or
Factor tree
Split the number into two factors. Keep splitting until every leaf (end of a branch) is prime.

Factor tree: 52

Greatest Common Factor (GCF)

The GCF is the largest whole number that divides evenly into every number in the group.

One way is to list all the factors and find the biggest one they share. That works, but it gets slow with bigger numbers.

!
Sanity check. The GCF is never bigger than the smallest number in the group: it has to fit into that number.

The fast way: use the prime factorizations

Factor each number, then take each prime the lists share, using its lowest power.

20 = 22 × 5     30 = 2 × 3 × 5both share a 2 and a 5, so the GCF of 20 and 30 is 2 × 5 = 10

With exponents: shared primes, lowest power

Give each prime its own column. Keep only the primes that are in every number, each at its lowest power.

Example 3

GCF by prime factorization

Use prime factorization to determine the greatest common factor.

(a) 24 and 42
24 = 23 × 3
42 = 2 × 3 × 7
Shared: 21 and 31.
GCF = 2 × 3 = 6
(b) 45 and 54
45 = 32 × 5
54 = 2 × 33
Shared: 32 only.
GCF = 9
(c) 32, 64, and 96
32 = 25, 64 = 26, 96 = 25 × 3
Shared: 2 at its lowest power, 25.
GCF = 32

Lowest Common Multiple (LCM)

The LCM is the smallest whole number that every number in the group divides into evenly.

The warm-up found one: list the multiples of 4 and of 6, and the first number on both lists is 12. That’s the slow way:

!
Multiplying gives a common multiple, not always the lowest. 4 × 6 = 24, but 4 and 6 meet first at 12.

The fast way: every prime, highest power

Factor each number, then take every prime that appears in either list, using its highest power.

14 = 2 × 7     21 = 3 × 7every prime once: LCM of 14 and 21 = 2 × 3 × 7 = 42

!
Sanity check. The LCM is never smaller than the largest number in the group: they all have to fit into it.

Putting GCF and LCM to Work

Both come from the same prime factorizations, so the real work in a word problem is choosing.

GCF largest equal groups
  • split things into the largest equal groups
  • identical bags, kits, bracelets…
  • nothing left over
12 yellow and 18 red bulbs → 6 boxes
LCM line up again
  • two repeating events
  • when do they happen at the same time again?
  • flashes, laps, buses, schedules
lighthouses, every 4 s and 6 s → 12 s

Together again: that’s an LCM

Two lights repeat on different cycles. They are together at the common multiples of the two cycles, and the first time is the LCM.

Examples 5 and 6

Applications

Solve each problem. State whether you need the GCF or the LCM before you calculate.

5 (GCF). Maya has 36 blue beads and 48 red beads. She wants identical bracelets, using all the beads, with the greatest number of bracelets possible.

(a) How many bracelets can Maya make?
Identical bracelets using every bead → GCF.
36 = 22 × 32, 48 = 24 × 3
GCF = 22 × 3 = 12
12 bracelets
(b) How many blue and how many red beads go on each bracelet?
Blue: 36 ÷ 12 = 3   Red: 48 ÷ 12 = 4
Check: 12 × 3 = 36, 12 × 4 = 48 ✓

6 (LCM). Two spotlights sweep a stage. Light A returns to its start every 18 seconds, Light B every 24 seconds. They start together.

(a) After how many seconds are both back at the start together?
Repeating events lining up → LCM.
18 = 2 × 32, 24 = 23 × 3
LCM = 23 × 32 = 72
72 seconds
(b) How many minutes and seconds is that?
72 ÷ 60 = 1 remainder 12
1 minute 12 seconds
✓

Key ideas

Prime: only 1 and itself as factors. Composite: more than two factors. 1 is neither, and 2 is the only even prime.
Prime factorization: ladder or tree, you always get the same primes. 52 = 2 × 2 × 13 = 22 × 13.
GCF: shared primes, lowest power. LCM: every prime, highest power. 20 and 30: GCF = 10, LCM = 60.
Which one? Largest equal groups, nothing left over → GCF. Repeating events lining up again → LCM.
