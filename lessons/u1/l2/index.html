Review: Operations with Fractions and Decimals

Building, simplifying and computing with fractions, then switching between fractions and decimals. A fraction and its decimal are the same number written two ways.

I can…
  • build equivalent fractions and simplify a fraction to lowest terms
  • add and subtract fractions (and mixed numbers) using a common denominator
  • multiply fractions straight across, and divide by multiplying by the reciprocal
  • convert a fraction to a decimal (it terminates or repeats) and a decimal to a fraction
  • use the order of operations with fractions and decimals
Grade 9 review Fractions, decimals and the order of operations: the number skills behind every unit this year.
1

Equivalent Fractions and Simplifying

In \dfrac{a}{b}, the denominator b cuts a whole into b equal pieces and the numerator a counts the pieces.

Equivalent fraction: multiply both parts by the same number (not 0).
\dfrac{2}{3}=\dfrac{2\times4}{3\times4}=\dfrac{8}{12}
Lowest terms: divide both parts by their greatest common factor (GCF), the biggest number that divides both. Stop when the only common factor left is 1.
\dfrac{8}{12}=\dfrac{8\div4}{12\div4}=\dfrac{2}{3}

Simplifying: undo it with the GCF

Simplifying runs the other way: group the pieces. The biggest group that works for both numbers is the GCF.

Example 1

Build up, then simplify

(a) Fill in the missing numerator to make an equivalent fraction. (b) Simplify to lowest terms.

(a)(i) \dfrac{3}{4}=\dfrac{\square}{12}
12\div4=3, so \times3 on both parts:
\dfrac{3\times3}{4\times3}=\dfrac{\boldsymbol{9}}{12}
(a)(ii) \dfrac{5}{6}=\dfrac{\square}{18}
18\div6=3, so \times3 on both parts:
\dfrac{5\times3}{6\times3}=\dfrac{\boldsymbol{15}}{18}
(b)(i) \dfrac{8}{12}
GCF(8,\ 12)=4
\dfrac{8\div4}{12\div4}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{2}{3}}
(b)(ii) \dfrac{10}{15}
GCF(10,\ 15)=5
\dfrac{10\div5}{15\div5}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{2}{3}}
(b)(iii) \dfrac{18}{24}
GCF(18,\ 24)=6
\dfrac{18\div6}{24\div6}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{3}{4}}
!
Not the biggest common factor? That’s OK, just divide again. \dfrac{18}{24}=\dfrac{9}{12}=\dfrac{3}{4} (÷2, then ÷3) gets the same answer as dividing by 6 once.
2

Adding and Subtracting Fractions

Fractions add or subtract only once they share a common denominator (same-size pieces). The LCD (the lowest common denominator) is the smallest number both denominators divide into.

1Find the LCD
2Rewrite each fraction with it
3Combine the numerators only (the denominator stays)
4Simplify

\dfrac{1}{4}+\dfrac{1}{3}=\dfrac{3}{12}+\dfrac{4}{12}=\dfrac{7}{12}

!
Never add the denominators. \dfrac{1}{4}+\dfrac{1}{3} is not \dfrac{2}{7}: that’s less than \dfrac{1}{3} on its own!

Mixed numbers

With mixed numbers, handle the whole-number parts and the fraction parts separately, then recombine.

Example 2

Common denominators

Evaluate. Give each answer in lowest terms.

(a) \dfrac{1}{4}+\dfrac{1}{6}
LCD of 4 and 6 is 12.
\dfrac{1}{4}+\dfrac{1}{6}=\dfrac{3}{12}+\dfrac{2}{12}
=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{5}{12}} (already lowest terms)
(b) \dfrac{3}{5}-\dfrac{1}{4}
LCD of 5 and 4 is 20.
\dfrac{3}{5}-\dfrac{1}{4}=\dfrac{12}{20}-\dfrac{5}{20}
=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{7}{20}} (7 and 20 share no factor)
(c) 2\dfrac{1}{3}+1\dfrac{1}{2}
Wholes: 2+1=3
Fractions: \dfrac{1}{3}+\dfrac{1}{2}=\dfrac{2}{6}+\dfrac{3}{6}=\dfrac{5}{6}
Recombine: \boldsymbol{3\dfrac{5}{6}}
3

Multiplying and Dividing Fractions

Multiply: go straight across: numerators together, denominators together. No common denominator needed.
Cancel shared factors first to keep the numbers small: \dfrac{3}{4}\times\dfrac{2}{9}=\dfrac{1}{2}\times\dfrac{1}{3}=\dfrac{1}{6}
Divide: multiply by the reciprocal (flip) of the second fraction.
\dfrac{a}{b}\div\dfrac{c}{d}=\dfrac{a}{b}\times\dfrac{d}{c}
Mixed numbers? Convert them to improper fractions first.

Dividing: how many scoops fit?

a\div b asks “how many b-size scoops fit into a?” That’s why dividing by a small fraction gives a bigger answer, and why it turns into multiplying by the reciprocal.

Mixed numbers become improper fractions

Cut every whole into pieces the size of the denominator, then count all the pieces: 2\dfrac{1}{4}=\dfrac{2\times4+1}{4}=\dfrac{9}{4}.

Example 3

Multiply and divide

Evaluate. Cancel common factors before multiplying where you can.

(a) \dfrac{2}{3}\times\dfrac{3}{8}
Cancel the 3s, and 2 into 8:
\dfrac{1}{1}\times\dfrac{1}{4}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{1}{4}}
Check: straight across, \dfrac{6}{24}=\dfrac{1}{4} ✓
(b) \dfrac{5}{8}\div\dfrac{3}{4}
Multiply by the reciprocal:
\dfrac{5}{8}\times\dfrac{4}{3}; cancel 4 into 8:
\dfrac{5}{2}\times\dfrac{1}{3}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{5}{6}}
(c) 2\dfrac{1}{4}\times\dfrac{2}{3}
Improper first: 2\dfrac{1}{4}=\dfrac{9}{4}
\dfrac{9}{4}\times\dfrac{2}{3}=\dfrac{18}{12}=\dfrac{3}{2}
=\boldsymbol{1\dfrac{1}{2}}

Converting Between Fractions and Decimals

Fraction → decimal: divide the numerator by the denominator.
\dfrac{3}{4}=3\div4=0.75
Decimal → fraction: the place value of the last digit is the denominator. Then simplify.
0.6=\dfrac{6}{10}=\dfrac{3}{5}

Some quotients end: they terminate. Others, like \dfrac{1}{3}=0.333\ldots, never do: they repeat. A bar marks the digits that repeat: 0.333\ldots=0.\overline{3}.

Decimal → fraction: read the place value

Say the decimal out loud with its place value: 0.45 is “forty-five hundredths”, so it is \dfrac{45}{100}. Then simplify.

Example 4

Two ways to write a number

(a) Convert each fraction to a decimal. (b) Convert each decimal to a fraction in lowest terms.

(a)(i) \dfrac{3}{8}
3\div8:
\dfrac{3}{8}=\boldsymbol{0.375}
It terminates.
(a)(ii) \dfrac{4}{5}
4\div5:
\dfrac{4}{5}=\boldsymbol{0.8}
It terminates.
(b)(i) 0.45
Last digit: hundredths.
0.45=\dfrac{45}{100}=\dfrac{45\div5}{100\div5}
=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{9}{20}}
(b)(ii) 0.85
Last digit: hundredths.
0.85=\dfrac{85}{100}=\dfrac{85\div5}{100\div5}
=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{17}{20}}
(c) Convert \dfrac{2}{3} to a decimal. What do you notice?
2\div3=0.6666\ldots: the 6 never stops, so \dfrac{2}{3}=\boldsymbol{0.\overline{6}} (“zero point six repeating”). The bar shows the digit that repeats forever. It repeats instead of terminating: each step of 2\div3 leaves the same remainder 2, so the same digit comes back forever. (If a division comes out even, it terminates.) Rounded for practical work, \dfrac{2}{3}\approx0.67.
5

Order of Operations with Fractions and Decimals

The order never changes: Brackets, Exponents, Multiplication and Division left to right, then Addition and Subtraction left to right. A product hiding inside a sum is done first.

Decimal rules

Adding or subtracting: line up the decimal points (fill empty places with 0).
Multiplying: multiply as whole numbers, then count the decimal places in the factors and put that many in the answer.
Dividing by a decimal: multiply both numbers by 10 until the divisor is a whole number: 1.2\div0.3=12\div3=4.
Example 5

The order still matters

Use the correct order of operations.

(a) Evaluate \dfrac{3}{4}-\dfrac{1}{2}\times\dfrac{1}{3}.
Multiplication before subtraction:
\dfrac{1}{2}\times\dfrac{1}{3}=\dfrac{1}{6}
\dfrac{3}{4}-\dfrac{1}{6}=\dfrac{9}{12}-\dfrac{2}{12}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{7}{12}}
(b) A muffin recipe calls for \dfrac{2}{3} cup of sugar for one full batch. Jordan wants to make half a batch. How much sugar should he use?
“Half of” means \times\dfrac{1}{2}:
\dfrac{1}{2}\times\dfrac{2}{3}=\dfrac{2}{6}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{1}{3}}
Jordan should use \dfrac{1}{3} of a cup of sugar.

Key ideas

Equivalent fractions. Multiply or divide top and bottom by the same number. Lowest terms: divide by the GCF.
Add and subtract with a common denominator: combine the numerators only. Mixed numbers: wholes and fractions separately.
Multiply straight across (cancel first). Divide: multiply by the reciprocal. Mixed numbers become improper fractions first.
Fractions and decimals are the same numbers written two ways: divide to get the decimal (it terminates or repeats); use place value to get the fraction.
