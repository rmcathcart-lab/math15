Graphing a Line Using Slope–Intercept Form

Turning the two numbers m and b into a drawn line, with no table of values. b tells you where to start; m tells you how to step.

I can…
  • read the slope m and the y-intercept b straight from y=mx+b
  • Step 1: plot the y-intercept (0,\,b) first
  • Step 2: write m as \dfrac{\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{\text{rise}}}{\textcolor{#c27a0e}{\text{run}}} and step to a second point
  • Step 3: draw a straight line through both points, extended across the grid
  • graph lines with negative slopes (step down) and fractional slopes (step right by the bottom number)

1

Reading m and b from the Equation

Any equation written as y=mx+b is in slope–intercept form: m is the slope and b is the y-intercept, the height at which the line crosses the y-axis. In y=4x+7, m=4 and b=7. Those two numbers are all you need to graph the line.
y=\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{4}x+\textcolor{#12805a}{7}
slope m=4
the hop
y-intercept b=7
the start

Remember the frogs?

Each frog is a line. Where it starts is b; its hop is the slope m.

Spot m and b

m is the number in front of x. b is the number on its own. Each one keeps its sign.

2

Step 1 — Plot the y-Intercept

Every graph starts the same way: plot \textcolor{#12805a}{(0,\,b)}. It sits on the y-axis (since x=0), it is guaranteed to be on the line, and it needs no calculation: just read b off the equation.
Example 1

Step 1 only

Consider the equation y=2x+5.

(a) State the slope m and the y-intercept b.
y=2x+5 is already in the form y=mx+b, so read the two numbers off directly:
m=\boldsymbol{2}\qquad b=\boldsymbol{5}
(b) Plot the y-intercept on the grid. This is Step 1: do not plot anything else yet.
The y-intercept is the point (0,b)=\boldsymbol{(0,5)}: go 5 units up the y-axis and mark it.
3

Step 2 — Use the Slope to Find a Second Point

One point will not determine a line; you need two. Write m as \dfrac{\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{\text{rise}}}{\textcolor{#c27a0e}{\text{run}}}, then from the y-intercept move right by the run and up or down by the rise (up if the rise is positive, down if it is negative). Where you land is a second point.
i
A whole number is itself over 1. 3=\dfrac{3}{1}: rise 3, run 1. So right 1, up 3.
Example 2

Step 2: a second point

Consider y=3x+1. The y-intercept (0,1) is already plotted as your Step 1 starting point.

(a) Write the slope m=3 as a fraction \dfrac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}.
Any whole number is itself over 1:
m=3=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{3}{1}}=\dfrac{\text{rise }3}{\text{run }1}: run right 1, rise up 3.
(b) Starting at (0,1), use your fraction from (a) to move to a second point. Plot that point and state its coordinates.
From (0,1): right 1 takes x from 0 to 1; up 3 takes y from 1 to 4.
Second point: \boldsymbol{(1,4)}
!
Don’t swap them. The rise (top) goes up or down; the run (bottom) goes right. \tfrac{3}{1} is up 3, right 1, not up 1, right 3.
4

Putting It Together

Step 1
Plot \textcolor{#12805a}{(0,\,b)}.
Step 2
Write m as \dfrac{\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{\text{rise}}}{\textcolor{#c27a0e}{\text{run}}}. Step right by the run, up or down by the rise. Plot that second point.
Step 3
Draw a straight line through the two points, extending it across the grid in both directions.
Example 3

All three steps

Graph the line y=2x-4.

(a) State the slope and the y-intercept.
y=2x-4: m=\boldsymbol{2}=\dfrac{2}{1}\qquad b=\boldsymbol{-4}
(b) Plot the y-intercept, use the slope to plot a second point, then draw the line through both points, extending it across the grid.
Step 1: plot (0,-4).
Step 2: right 1, up 2 \Rightarrow\ \boldsymbol{(1,-2)}.
Step 3: rule a line through both and extend it both ways.
Check: at x=3, y=2(3)-4=2, and the line does pass through (3,2) ✓
Example 4

A steeper line

Graph the line y=4x-3.

(a) State the slope and the y-intercept.
y=4x-3: m=\boldsymbol{4}=\dfrac{4}{1}\qquad b=\boldsymbol{-3}
(b) Plot the y-intercept, use the slope to plot a second point, then draw the line through both points, extending it across the grid.
Step 1: plot (0,-3).
Step 2: right 1, up 4 \Rightarrow\ \boldsymbol{(1,1)}.
Step 3: draw the line through both points. It is steeper than Example 3 because 4>2.

Three ways it goes wrong (and how to catch them)

After you draw a line, check one more point: read a point off your line and put its x into the equation. If the y doesn’t match, something slipped.

5

Negative and Fractional Slopes

The same three steps work for every slope. If m is negative, write it as \dfrac{-\text{rise}}{\text{run}} and step right and down. If m is already a fraction such as \tfrac{2}{3}, nothing needs converting: step right by the denominator and up (or down) by the numerator.
-2=\dfrac{\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{-2}}{\textcolor{#c27a0e}{1}}
The minus sign rides on the rise: right 1, down 2. The line falls from left to right.
\dfrac{\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{2}}{\textcolor{#c27a0e}{3}}
Top is the rise, bottom is the run: right 3, up 2. No decimals needed.

Negative slopes go downhill

Fraction slopes: the bottom number is the run

Example 5

A negative slope and a fractional slope

Graph each line using the three steps.

(a) y=-2x+4
m=-2=\dfrac{-2}{1}, b=4. From (0,4) go right 1, down 2 to (1,2). Draw the line through both.
(b) y=\dfrac{2}{3}x-3
m=\dfrac{2}{3}, b=-3. From (0,-3) go right 3, up 2 to (3,-1). Draw the line through both.

Make your own line

✓

Key ideas

Read the two numbers. In y=\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{m}x+\textcolor{#12805a}{b}, m (in front of x) is the slope and b (on its own) is the y-intercept. Each keeps its sign.
Step 1: start at (0,\,b). It is on the y-axis, it is on the line, and it needs no calculation.
Step 2: step the slope. Write m as \tfrac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}} (a whole number is over 1). Go right by the run, up by a positive rise or down by a negative rise.
Step 3: join and extend. Rule a line through the points, across the whole grid. Check: a point on your line must fit the equation.
