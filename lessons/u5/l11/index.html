Interpreting and Sketching Real-World Graphs

Every real-world graph tells a story. Today we read that story one piece at a time: where the graph rises, falls or stays flat, and how steep it is. Then we go the other way: read a story and sketch its graph.

I can…
  • read a graph left to right and say what is happening in each interval: increasing, decreasing or constant
  • tell a steep piece (fast change) from a gradual one (slow change), and find a rate of change with units
  • match a written story to its graph, and explain why the other graphs don’t fit
  • sketch a graph with no scale from a written story
  • finish a graph that shows only part of a story
RF1 Interpret and explain the relationships among data, graphs and situations.

The big one. Eli graphed his trip to school. How long did he take to cover the red part? Guess first.
A 3 min
B 1 min
C 10 s
D 0 s
D, 0 seconds. The red part is vertical: it starts and ends at 3 min. At 3 minutes the graph says Eli is at 200 m and at 600 m: in two places at once! That’s teleporting, so the graph is impossible.
A real trip takes some time, so the red part must lean to the right, for example from (3, 200) to (4, 600) for a 1-minute bus ride.

Argue with your neighbour: could Eli’s trip really happen? What would he have to do?

1

Reading the Shape of a Graph

Read a graph left to right, one interval (piece) at a time, the way time moves. In each interval the quantity is:

increasing
the graph rises
decreasing
the graph falls
constant
the graph is flat: no change at all
steep = fast
gradual = slow
The steepness of a piece is its rate of change: a steep piece changes fast, a gradual piece changes slowly.
On a distance from home graph: rising = moving away, flat = stopped, falling = coming back home. Careful: on a speed graph, flat means a steady speed; only flat at 0 means stopped.

Watch the warm-up trips happen, in step with their graphs.

Think Could a real distance-from-home graph ever have a perfectly vertical piece? What about a very steep one?
Never vertical: a vertical piece puts you in two places at the same moment (like Eli). A very steep piece is fine: a fast trip (a bus, a car) still takes a little time, so the piece leans slightly to the right.

How fast? The rate of change

To compare steepness with numbers, find the rate of change of a piece:

\text{rate of change}=\dfrac{\text{change in the quantity (up or down)}}{\text{change in time (across)}}
The units are “quantity per time”: km/h per minute, cm per minute, m per minute. Rising pieces have a positive rate, falling pieces a negative rate, flat pieces a rate of 0.
!
Steeper means faster, not higher. To find the fastest change, compare the size of the rates and ignore the sign: a rate of -12 is a faster change than a rate of 6.
Example 1

Jae’s bike ride: a speed graph

Jae rides her bicycle from home to the library along a route with one stop sign. The graph shows her speed at every moment of the 18-minute ride, made of five segments meeting at A to F.

(a) With reference to Jae’s ride, explain what is happening during each interval.

(i) A to B
The graph rises from 0 to 24 km/h over the first 4 minutes: Jae is starting from rest and steadily speeding up until she reaches her cruising speed of 24 km/h.
(ii) C to D
The graph falls steeply from 24 km/h to 0 between 9 and 11 minutes: Jae is braking, slowing down and coming to a complete stop at the stop sign.
(iii) E to F
The graph rises again from 0 to 20 km/h over the last 6 minutes: after the stop Jae pulls away and speeds up again, but only to 20 km/h, a bit slower than before.
(b) Determine the rate of change, in km/h per minute, between C and D.
C=(9,\ 24) and D=(11,\ 0).
\text{rate}=\dfrac{0-24}{11-9}=\dfrac{-24}{2}=\boldsymbol{-12} km/h per minute.
The negative sign means her speed is dropping by 12 km/h each minute.
(c) Which single segment shows the fastest rate of change (steepest), and what does this tell you about Jae at that time?
Compare the three slanted segments: A\text{–}B:\ \tfrac{24}{4}=6; C\text{–}D:\ \tfrac{-24}{2}=-12; E\text{–}F:\ \tfrac{20}{6}\approx 3.3.
C to D is the steepest (size 12). Jae is braking hard: her speed changes fastest as she stops for the stop sign.
(d) For how many minutes, in total, was Jae’s speed constant?
Flat segments only:
B to C: 9-4=5 min (cruising at 24 km/h)
D to E: 12-11=1 min (stopped: constant at 0)
Total: 5+1=\boldsymbol{6} minutes
Rates on the calculator. Part (b): (−)24÷2= gives -12. Part (c), E to F: 20÷6= gives 3.333…, about 3.3.

Example 1 check: ride along with Jae

The bike, the speedometer and the graph move together. Check your answers to part (a).

!
Flat doesn’t always mean stopped. Read the axis label first. On a speed graph, flat and high means moving at a steady speed; only flat at 0 means stopped. On a distance graph, any flat piece means stopped.

Sorting the pieces

To answer “for how long was it increasing (or constant)?”, sort the pieces first, then add up their times.

2

Matching a Written Story to a Graph

Break the story into phases, in order. Then rule out any graph whose shape doesn’t fit a phase.

a stop or a pause→ a flat piece
“faster”→ a steeper piece
“coming back”→ a decreasing piece
Example 2

Which graph is Sam’s walk?

Story: Sam leaves home and walks at a steady pace toward school. Partway there, he stops at a corner to talk to a friend for a few minutes. Then he jogs the rest of the way to school, covering the remaining distance faster than he walked the first part. Each graph shows Sam’s distance from home versus time.

(a) Which graph matches Sam’s story?
The story has three phases: a steady walk (rising), a stop (flat), then a faster jog (rising more steeply). Only A shows gentle rise → flat → steeper rise.
Answer: A
(b) Explain, in one sentence each, why the other three graphs do not match.
B: the first piece is steeper than the last, so Sam would have walked faster than he jogged.
C: the middle piece goes down: Sam heads back toward home instead of stopping.
D: the flat parts come first and last: he waits before leaving and stops at the end, instead of pausing partway.

Example 2 check: phase by phase, rule them out

3

Sketching a Graph From a Written Story

With no scale, only the shape matters. Exact numbers don’t matter.

1Choose the axes. Time usually goes across. Label both axes.
2Split the story into phases, in order.
3Each phase: increasing, decreasing or constant? Steep or gradual?
4Join the pieces into one continuous graph, left to right.

Straight or curved?

A steady rate makes a straight piece. If the change gets faster or slower, the piece curves. Name each shape first, then tap it.

Example 3

From a story to a sketch

Sketch a graph with no scale for each situation. Label your axes.

(a) Height of water in a bottle: a tap fills the bottle at a steady rate from empty until it is full, and then the tap is shut off and the bottle sits full and unchanged.
A straight rising piece (steady filling) that turns flat the moment the tap is shut off: the height then stays constant.
(b) Temperature of a mug of hot cocoa left on a table: it cools quickly at first, then cools more and more slowly as it gets close to room temperature (never quite reaching it).
A curve that falls steeply at first and then flattens out, getting closer and closer to room temperature without ever touching it.

Example 3 check: one phrase, one piece

Each phrase of the story becomes one piece of the graph. Watch the scene and the sketch together.

4

Combining Reading and Sketching

Some questions graph only part of a story. You read that part (often its rate of change), then continue the sketch for the rest of the story on the same grid.

Example 4

Mia’s bath

Mia runs a bath. For the first 6 minutes the tap fills the tub at a steady rate and the depth rises from 0 cm to 24 cm (already drawn below). She then soaks for 10 minutes, during which the depth stays about the same. Finally she pulls the plug and the tub drains at a steady rate, from 24 cm back to 0 cm over the next 8 minutes.

(a) What is the rate of change of the water depth during the first 6 minutes (while the tap is filling the tub)?
The depth goes from 0 cm to 24 cm in 6 minutes:
\text{rate}=\dfrac{24-0}{6-0}=\dfrac{24}{6}=\boldsymbol{4} cm per minute. Positive, because the tub is filling.
(b) On the same grid, continue the sketch to show the rest of Mia’s bath (the soak, then the drain).
Soak: a flat piece at 24 cm from 6 to 16 minutes (10 minutes, depth unchanged).
Drain: a straight piece falling from (16,\ 24) to (24,\ 0) over 8 minutes: a rate of \tfrac{-24}{8}=-3 cm per minute, so it is a little less steep than the filling part.

Example 4 check: fill, soak, drain

Example 5

The drive to the cabin

A family drives from home to a mountain cabin. Sketch their speed versus time for the whole trip.

  • They pull out of the driveway and speed up steadily to highway speed.
  • They cruise at that highway speed for a long stretch.
  • They slow down and come to a complete stop at a gas station, where they stay stopped for a while to refuel.
  • After refuelling, they speed up again, but only back up to a slightly slower cruising speed than before (more traffic near the cabin).
  • Finally, they slow down and come to a stop as they arrive at the cabin.
Left to right: rising (speeding up), flat and high (highway), falling to 0 then flat along the axis (stopped at the gas station), rising to a lower flat level (slower cruise), and falling to 0 (arriving). Only the shape matters.

Example 5 check: drive to the cabin

The same phrase-by-phrase method, with a speedometer to watch.

✓

Key ideas

Read left to right, one piece at a time: rising = increasing, falling = decreasing, flat = constant (no change).
Steep = fast, gradual = slow. \text{rate of change}=\dfrac{\text{change in quantity}}{\text{change in time}}, with units like cm per minute. Compare sizes, ignore the sign.
Matching: split the story into phases. A stop is flat, “faster” is steeper, “coming back” is decreasing. Rule out graphs that don’t fit.
Sketching: label the axes, one piece per phase, steady = straight, changing speed = curved. Read the axis label: flat on a speed graph is not stopped!
