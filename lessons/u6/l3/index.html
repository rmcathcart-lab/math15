Measuring the Length of a Line Segment: Diagonal Segments and the Distance Formula

Most segments on a grid are diagonal: no row of squares to count. But every diagonal segment hides a right triangle. Its two legs are the run and the rise, and the segment is the hypotenuse. Pythagoras does the rest, and written with coordinates it becomes the distance formula.

I can…
  • draw the right triangle under a diagonal segment and name the coordinates of its right-angle corner
  • find the run and the rise by subtracting coordinates
  • use the Pythagorean theorem to find the length of a diagonal segment
  • use the distance formula d=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}, with or without a grid
  • give a length exactly (in simplest form, like 3\sqrt5) and as a decimal, and use a map scale

1

Turning a Diagonal Segment into a Right Triangle

Segment AB runs diagonally, from A(1,\ 1) to B(5,\ 4). To measure it, build a right triangle under it. Add a third point C that takes its x-coordinate from B and its y-coordinate from A: C(5,\ 1). The right angle is at C.

The horizontal leg AC is the run and the vertical leg CB is the rise, found just like in Lesson 2: \text{run}=5-1=4 and \text{rise}=4-1=3.

AB is the hypotenuse, so
(AB)^2=(\textcolor{#c27a0e}{\text{run}})^2+(\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{\text{rise}})^2=4^2+3^2=25\quad\Longrightarrow\quad AB=\sqrt{25}=5
!
Don’t add the run and the rise. 4+3=7 is the truck’s trip along the two legs (the long way round). The diagonal is the drone’s short cut: shorter than run + rise, but longer than either leg.

Finding the corner

The right-angle corner sits straight across from one endpoint and straight up or down from the other. So it borrows one coordinate from each end: the x from one, the y from the other.

Example 1

Build the triangle under CD

The diagonal segment CD is graphed below, from C(-1,\ -2) to D(3,\ 1).

(a) On the grid, draw the horizontal leg and the vertical leg needed to form a right triangle with hypotenuse CD. Label the right-angle vertex E.
Across from C to E (the run), then up from E to D (the rise). The right angle is at E.
(b) State the coordinates of the right-angle vertex E you drew.
E takes its x from D and its y from C: \boldsymbol{E(3,\ -2)}
(c) Find the length of the run (horizontal leg) and the rise (vertical leg).
run =x_2-x_1=3-(-1)=\boldsymbol{4}
rise =y_2-y_1=1-(-2)=\boldsymbol{3}
(d) Use the Pythagorean theorem to calculate the exact length of CD.
(CD)^2=4^2+3^2=16+9=25
CD=\sqrt{25}=\boldsymbol{5} units
Think Could the corner go above the segment instead, at (-1,\ 1)? Would CD come out different?
Yes, that corner works too (its x comes from C, its y from D). The triangle is flipped, but the legs are still 4 and 3, so CD=5 either way.
2

Deriving the Distance Formula

Do the same construction with letters instead of numbers. For P(x_1,\ y_1) and Q(x_2,\ y_2), the corner is R(x_2,\ y_1), so \text{run}=x_2-x_1 and \text{rise}=y_2-y_1. PQ is the hypotenuse, so (PQ)^2=(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2. Take the square root:

The Distance Formula. The distance between (x_1,\ y_1) and (x_2,\ y_2) is
d=\sqrt{(\textcolor{#c27a0e}{x_2-x_1})^2+(\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{y_2-y_1})^2}
run squared, plus rise squared, then the square root. It is just Pythagoras written with coordinates.
!
The square root covers the whole sum. \sqrt{16+9}=\sqrt{25}=5. Taking the roots separately gives \sqrt{16}+\sqrt{9}=4+3=7: that’s adding the run and the rise again (the truck!). Add first, then take the root.

Either point can go first

It does not matter which point you call (x_1,\ y_1). Subtracting in the other order only flips the signs, and squaring erases them.

Example 2

Straight into the formula

Use the distance formula to determine the exact length of each segment. No grid needed: just substitute directly into d=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}.

(a) E(0,\ 0) to F(6,\ 8)
d=\sqrt{(6-0)^2+(8-0)^2}
=\sqrt{36+64}=\sqrt{100}
=\boldsymbol{10} units
(b) G(-3,\ 4) to H(1,\ 1)
d=\sqrt{(1-(-3))^2+(1-4)^2}
=\sqrt{4^2+(-3)^2}=\sqrt{16+9}=\sqrt{25}
=\boldsymbol{5} units
!
Brackets around a negative. 1-(-3)=1+3=4. And when you square a negative, keep its brackets: (-3)^2=9, never -9. A squared number is never negative.
Squaring a negative on the calculator. ((−)3)x²ENTER gives 9. Without the brackets, (−)3x² squares the 3 first and gives −9.
3

Applying the Distance Formula Directly

With the formula, no grid is needed at all. Label the two points (x_1,\ y_1) and (x_2,\ y_2), substitute, and simplify. If the number under the root isn’t a perfect square, write it as a mixed radical in simplest form, just like in Unit 1.

1Label the points
2Subtract: run and rise
3Square each
4Add
5Square root (simplify)
Example 3

Exact lengths

Use the distance formula to determine the exact length of each segment.

(a) I(0,\ 0) to J(5,\ 12)
d=\sqrt{(5-0)^2+(12-0)^2}
=\sqrt{25+144}=\sqrt{169}
=\boldsymbol{13} units
(b) K(2,\ 3) to L(5,\ 9)
d=\sqrt{(5-2)^2+(9-3)^2}
=\sqrt{9+36}=\sqrt{45}=\sqrt{9}\cdot\sqrt{5}
=\boldsymbol{3\sqrt{5}} units (\approx6.71)

Exact answers: a mixed radical in simplest form

Look for the biggest perfect square that divides the number under the root, and take its square root out front. If no perfect square (other than 1) divides it, the root is already in simplest form.

The decimal of a root. \sqrt{\phantom{x}} is 2ndx²: √45ENTER gives 6.708203932 → 6.71. Check your mixed radical gives the same decimal: 3×√5ENTER.
Example 4

Exact, then decimal

Line segment MN has endpoints M(-3,\ -2) and N(3,\ 0).

(a) Determine the exact length of MN, as a mixed radical in simplest form.
d=\sqrt{(3-(-3))^2+(0-(-2))^2}
=\sqrt{6^2+2^2}=\sqrt{36+4}=\sqrt{40}
=\sqrt{4}\cdot\sqrt{10}=\boldsymbol{2\sqrt{10}} units
(b) Determine the length of MN as a decimal, rounded to the nearest hundredth.
MN=2\sqrt{10}=2(3.16227\ldots)
=6.3245\ldots\approx\boldsymbol{6.32} units
Round only at the end. 2×√10ENTER gives 6.32455532, so 6.32. √40ENTER gives the same number: a good check of your simplifying.
4

A Real-World Application

The formula is especially useful when a map or floor plan sits on a coordinate grid: every place already has coordinates.

1Find the distance in grid units with the distance formula.
2Multiply by the map scale to get the real distance.
Example 5

The park map

A town places a coordinate grid over a park map. The picnic shelter is at (3,\ 8) and the swimming dock is at (11,\ 2), where each grid unit represents 25 m.

(a) Determine the distance between the picnic shelter and the swimming dock, in grid units.
d=\sqrt{(11-3)^2+(2-8)^2}
=\sqrt{64+36}=\sqrt{100}=\boldsymbol{10} grid units
(b) Use the map scale to convert your answer from part (a) into an actual distance, in metres.
10\text{ units}\times25\text{ m per unit}=\boldsymbol{250}\text{ m}
The shelter and the dock are 250 m apart.
Think The park paths only run east–west and north–south. How much farther is it to walk the paths from the shelter to the dock?
The paths are the run and the rise: 8+6=14 units =14\times25=350 m. That’s 100 m farther than the straight line (the truck vs. the drone again).
✓

Key ideas

Every diagonal segment hides a right triangle. The corner takes its x from one end and its y from the other. The run and rise are the legs; the segment is the hypotenuse.
The distance formula is Pythagoras with coordinates: d=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}. Subtract, square, add, then take the root of the whole sum.
Either point can go first. The other order only flips the signs, and squaring erases them. Keep brackets on negatives: (-3)^2=9.
Exact and decimal. Simplify the root: \sqrt{45}=3\sqrt5. Round only at the end: \approx6.71. On a map: grid units first, then × the scale.
