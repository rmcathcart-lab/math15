Measuring the Length of a Line Segment

A line segment is the piece of a line between two endpoints, so it has a length we can measure. When a segment is horizontal or vertical, we can count the grid squares, or skip the grid and just subtract the coordinates that change. A length is never negative, so we keep the size and drop the sign.

I can…
  • tell whether a segment is horizontal (same y) or vertical (same x) from its endpoints
  • find the length of a horizontal or vertical segment by counting grid squares
  • find the same length by subtracting coordinates: |x_2-x_1| or |y_2-y_1|
  • explain why a length uses the absolute value, and subtract negative coordinates correctly
  • find the perimeter of a rectangle drawn on a grid

1

Lines, Segments, and the Cartesian Plane

Every point on the Cartesian plane is named by an ordered pair (x,\ y): the x-coordinate is the left/right move from the origin, the y-coordinate the up/down move. A line runs on forever in both directions, but a line segment is only the piece of a line between two endpoints, written segment AB or \overline{AB}. Because a segment stops at both ends, it has a definite, measurable length.
2

Length of a Horizontal Segment

A segment is horizontal when both endpoints share the same y-coordinate. Find its length by counting grid squares, or by subtracting the x-coordinates of the endpoints. Subtracting in the wrong order gives a negative, and a length is always positive, so take the absolute value (or simply subtract the smaller x from the larger):
length =|x_2-x_1|
Example 1

Two horizontal segments

Two horizontal segments are graphed on the grid below.

(a) State which coordinate (x or y) is the same for both endpoints of each segment. What does that tell you about the segment?
The y-coordinate matches: y=3 for \overline{AB}, y=-3 for \overline{CD}. A shared y means the segment never rises or falls: it is horizontal, so its length comes from the x’s.
(b) State the coordinates of the endpoints of \overline{AB} and of \overline{CD}.
A(-4,\ 3) and B(3,\ 3); C(-5,\ -3) and D(1,\ -3)
(c) Find the length of each segment by counting grid squares.
\overline{AB}: 7 squares. \overline{CD}: 6 squares.
(d) For each segment, subtract the x-coordinate of the left endpoint from the x-coordinate of the right endpoint. Compare your results to part (c).
\overline{AB}: 3-(-4)=\boldsymbol{7}; \overline{CD}: 1-(-5)=\boldsymbol{6}. Exactly the same numbers as counting squares.
(e) Complete the rule: for a horizontal segment, length =|\underline{\hspace{1.5em}}-\underline{\hspace{1.5em}}|.
length =|x_2-x_1|: the two x-coordinates.

Why the absolute value?

Subtracting in one order or the other gives the same number with a different sign. The sign only tells you which way you walked. A length is a distance, so we keep the size: the absolute value |-14|=14.

Example 2

Lengths from coordinates

Determine each length. Show the subtraction you use.

(a) Segment TU has endpoints T(8,\ -3) and U(-6,\ -3). Subtracting one way gives 8-(-6)=14; the other way gives -6-8=-14. Explain why both may be used for the length, and state the length of TU.
The two subtractions differ only in sign: reversing the order measures the same gap in the opposite direction. A length is a distance, so keep the positive value:
TU=|8-(-6)|=|-6-8|=|\pm14|
TU=\boldsymbol{14} units
(b) Segment VW has endpoints V(k-4,\ m) and W(k+7,\ m). Determine the length of VW.
Both endpoints have y=m, so VW is horizontal: subtract the x’s.
VW=|(k+7)-(k-4)|
=|k+7-k+4|=|11|
VW=\boldsymbol{11} units. (The k’s cancel, so the length doesn’t depend on k.)
!
Minus a negative is plus. 8-(-6)=8+6=14, not 2. Write the brackets around the negative first, then change the two signs into one +.
Negatives on the calculator. Use the negative key (−), not the minus key: 8−(−)6ENTER gives 14. The other order, (−)6−8ENTER, gives −14: same size, opposite sign.

Coordinates with letters: the letters cancel

In Example 2(b) the endpoints contain a letter, k. Whatever number k stands for, the segment just slides along: its length never changes.

3

Length of a Vertical Segment

A segment is vertical when both endpoints share the same x-coordinate. The method is the same, turned 90^\circ: subtract the y-coordinates and take the absolute value, since a length can never be negative:
length =|y_2-y_1|
Notice the swap: a horizontal segment shares a y and uses the x’s; a vertical segment shares an x and uses the y’s.
horizontal
y’s match, use the x’s
vertical
x’s match, use the y’s

Same x or same y? Look before you subtract

Before any arithmetic, find the coordinate that matches. It tells you the direction, and the other coordinate gives the length.

Example 3

Two vertical segments

Two vertical segments are graphed on the grid below.

(a) State which coordinate (x or y) is the same for both endpoints of each segment. What does that tell you about the segment?
This time the x-coordinate matches: x=-3 for \overline{GH}, x=2 for \overline{IJ}. A shared x means the segment goes straight up and down: it is vertical, so its length comes from the y’s.
(b) State the coordinates of the endpoints of \overline{GH} and of \overline{IJ}.
G(-3,\ 4) and H(-3,\ -2); I(2,\ 5) and J(2,\ -3)
(c) Find the length of each segment by counting grid squares.
\overline{GH}: 6 squares. \overline{IJ}: 8 squares.
(d) For each segment, subtract the y-coordinate of the lower endpoint from the y-coordinate of the upper endpoint. Compare your results to part (c).
\overline{GH}: 4-(-2)=\boldsymbol{6}; \overline{IJ}: 5-(-3)=\boldsymbol{8}. The same as counting squares.
(e) Complete the rule: for a vertical segment, length =|\underline{\hspace{1.5em}}-\underline{\hspace{1.5em}}|.
length =|y_2-y_1|: the two y-coordinates.
Example 4

Vertical lengths from coordinates

Determine each length.

(a) Segment KL has endpoints K(5,\ -8) and L(5,\ 6). Determine the length of KL.
Both x’s are 5, so KL is vertical: subtract the y’s.
KL=|6-(-8)|=|6+8|=|14|
KL=\boldsymbol{14} units
(b) Segment MN has endpoints M(h,\ n-5) and N(h,\ n+9). Determine the length of MN.
Both x’s are h, so MN is vertical:
MN=|(n+9)-(n-5)|
=|n+9-n+5|=|14|
MN=\boldsymbol{14} units
4

Applying Both Skills: Perimeter on a Grid

Shapes drawn on a grid often have only horizontal and vertical sides, so the perimeter (the distance around the outside) is just the sum of the side lengths found by the two rules above. In a rectangle opposite sides are equal, so
\text{Perimeter}=2(\text{horizontal})+2(\text{vertical})
For example, sides of 6 and 4 give 2(6)+2(4)=20 units.
Example 5

Perimeter of a rectangle

Rectangle ABCD is graphed on the grid below.

(a) State the coordinates of all four vertices.
A(-4,\ -2), B(5,\ -2), C(5,\ 4), D(-4,\ 4)
(b) Identify which sides are horizontal and which are vertical.
Horizontal: \overline{AB} (both y=-2) and \overline{DC} (both y=4).
Vertical: \overline{BC} (both x=5) and \overline{AD} (both x=-4).
(c) Find the length of each of the four sides.
AB=|5-(-4)|=\boldsymbol{9}, DC=|5-(-4)|=\boldsymbol{9}
BC=|4-(-2)|=\boldsymbol{6}, AD=|4-(-2)|=\boldsymbol{6}
Opposite sides are equal, as they must be in a rectangle.
(d) Calculate the perimeter of rectangle ABCD.
P=2(\text{horizontal})+2(\text{vertical})
P=2(9)+2(6)=18+12
P=\boldsymbol{30} units. Check: 9+6+9+6=30 ✓
✓

Key ideas

Segment vs line. A line goes on forever. A segment \overline{AB} stops at its two endpoints, so it has a length, written AB.
Horizontal (same y): length =|x_2-x_1|. Vertical (same x): length =|y_2-y_1|. The coordinate that matches plays no part.
Absolute value. Either order gives the same size; the sign is only the direction. A length is never negative. Careful: 3-(-4)=3+4=7.
Perimeter on a grid: find every side with the two rules and add. A rectangle: P=2(\text{horizontal})+2(\text{vertical}).
