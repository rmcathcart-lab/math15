Applications: Distance, Midpoint, and Finding a Missing Endpoint

Today the two formulas go to work on maps, trails and zip-lines. The distance formula tells us how far apart two places are. The midpoint formula tells us where halfway is. And we run the midpoint formula backward: if we know the middle and one end, we can find the other end.

I can…
  • choose the right tool: the distance formula for “how far?”, the midpoint formula for “where is halfway?”
  • find a missing endpoint from the midpoint and the other endpoint: double the midpoint, subtract the known endpoint
  • use a map scale to change grid units into metres or kilometres
  • say how far east or west and north or south one place is from another
  • use both formulas, one after the other, to solve a real problem

Warm-up

The Other End of the Zipline

Fog fills the canyon. You can see the start, S, and the flag at the exact middle of the cable, M. 1 square = 1 unit.

1
A rope lies on a number line. One end is at 2. Its exact middle is at 5. Where is the other end?
8. From 2 to the middle is 3 steps, so go 3 more: 5+3=8. Check: \frac{2+8}{2}=5.
2
On the map, from S to the flag M: how many squares right? How many down?
4 right and 3 down.
3
M is the exact middle. So from M to the hidden end, how far right? How far down?
The same again: 4 right and 3 down. The middle splits the cable into two equal halves.
!
The big one. S is (2,\ 9) and M is (6,\ 6). Where is the hidden end, E? Guess first.
A (4,\ 7.5)
B (8,\ 15)
C (10,\ 3)
D (4,\ 3)
C: E(10,\ 3). From M(6,\ 6) go 4 right and 3 down. Check: \left(\frac{2+10}{2},\ \frac{9+3}{2}\right)=(6,\ 6) ✓
A averaged S and M: that point is between S and the flag, on the wrong side! B added S and M and never subtracted: way up in the sky. D is the step (4,\ -3) with the sign lost, not a point.
!
Then: each square is 20 m. How long is the whole cable, from S to E? Guess first.
A 140 m
B 200 m
C 280 m
B: 200 m. The whole cable goes 8 right and 6 down: \sqrt{8^2+6^2}=\sqrt{100}=10 squares, and 10\times20=200 m. (280 m counts 8+6=14 squares along the grid lines, but a cable doesn’t turn corners. 140 m is only 7 squares.)

Pencil check: put the middle of your pencil on M and the eraser on S. Where does the tip point?

1

The Two Formulas

For two points P(x_1,\ y_1) and Q(x_2,\ y_2):

Distance — how far apart?
d=\sqrt{(\textcolor{#c27a0e}{x_2-x_1})^2+(\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{y_2-y_1})^2}
Subtract to get the run and the rise, square, add, square root.
Midpoint — where is halfway?
M\left(\dfrac{x_1+x_2}{2},\ \dfrac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)
Add and halve: the average of the x’s and the average of the y’s.
!
Subtract for distance, add for midpoint. Distance asks how far apart, so it uses the differences. The midpoint asks where the middle is, so it uses the average. Mixing them up is the most common slip.
2

Finding a Missing Endpoint

If we know the midpoint M and only one endpoint, put what we know into the midpoint formula and solve for the unknown coordinate: one small equation for x, one for y.

Shortcut: x_2=2x_M-x_1 and y_2=2y_M-y_1. In words: double the midpoint, subtract the known endpoint.
Worked demonstration. P(2,\ 5) is an endpoint and M(6,\ 9) is the midpoint. Solve each coordinate equation:
\dfrac{2+x_2}{2}=6\ \Rightarrow\ 2+x_2=12\ \Rightarrow\ x_2=10
\dfrac{5+y_2}{2}=9\ \Rightarrow\ 5+y_2=18\ \Rightarrow\ y_2=13
so \boldsymbol{Q(10,\ 13)}. Check by putting both endpoints back in: \left(\dfrac{2+10}{2},\ \dfrac{5+13}{2}\right)=(6,\ 9) ✓

Why “double the midpoint, subtract the known endpoint” works

Take one coordinate at a time. The midpoint formula says “add the two ends and halve”. To run it backward, undo each step: undo the ÷ 2 by doubling, then undo the + by subtracting the end you know.

Negatives on the calculator. Use the (−) key for a negative number and the − key for subtracting. Example 2(a): 2(-1)-(-6) is 2×(−)1−(−)6ENTER → 4.
Example 1

Find the other endpoint

A(4,\ 1) is one endpoint of \overline{AB} and the midpoint is M(9,\ 6). Determine the other endpoint, B.

Find B.
Double the midpoint, subtract the known endpoint:
x_B=2x_M-x_1=2(9)-4=18-4=\boldsymbol{14}
y_B=2y_M-y_1=2(6)-1=12-1=\boldsymbol{11}
\boldsymbol{B(14,\ 11)}
Think How can you check your answer?
Find the midpoint of A and your B. It must come out to M:
\left(\dfrac{4+14}{2},\ \dfrac{1+11}{2}\right)=\left(\dfrac{18}{2},\ \dfrac{12}{2}\right)=(9,\ 6) ✓
Example 2

Missing endpoints with negatives

Determine the coordinates of the missing endpoint in each case.

(a) E(-6,\ 3) is one endpoint of \overline{EF}, with midpoint M(-1,\ 7). Find F.
x_F=2(-1)-(-6)=-2+6=\boldsymbol{4}
y_F=2(7)-3=14-3=\boldsymbol{11}
\boldsymbol{F(4,\ 11)}
(b) G(5,\ -8) is one endpoint of \overline{GH}, with midpoint M(2,\ -3). Find H.
x_H=2(2)-5=4-5=\boldsymbol{-1}
y_H=2(-3)-(-8)=-6+8=\boldsymbol{2}
\boldsymbol{H(-1,\ 2)}
!
Brackets around a negative. Subtracting a negative is adding: -2-(-6)=-2+6=4. Write the brackets first, then simplify.

Three tempting answers, one right one

The midpoint is not an endpoint. To find the missing end, don’t average the end you know with the midpoint: go past the midpoint by the same amount again. Test each answer by checking that M is really in the middle.

3

Real-World Distance Applications

When a map or floor plan is laid on a coordinate grid, the distance formula gives real distances straight from the coordinates. Find the distance in grid units first, then multiply by the map scale to change it to metres or kilometres.

Map scale: “each grid unit represents 200 m” means 1 unit = 200 m. So 13 units =13\times200=2\,600 m. And 1\,000\text{ m}=1\text{ km}, so divide metres by 1\,000 to get kilometres.
The whole distance in one line. 2ndx² is \sqrt{\phantom{x}}: √(5x²+12x²)ENTER → 13. Close the bracket: the root covers the whole sum. Then ×200 for the scale.
Example 3

Search and rescue

A search-and-rescue team overlays a grid on a topographic map. The ranger station is at (3,\ 4) and a hiker’s radio signal is traced to (8,\ 16), where each grid unit represents 200 m.

(a) Determine the distance between the ranger station and the hiker’s signal, in grid units.
d=\sqrt{(8-3)^2+(16-4)^2}
=\sqrt{5^2+12^2}=\sqrt{25+144}
=\sqrt{169}=\boldsymbol{13} grid units
(b) Use the map scale to convert your answer from part (a) into an actual distance, in kilometres.
13\text{ units}\times200\text{ m per unit}=2\,600 m
2\,600\div1\,000=\boldsymbol{2.6} km
The hiker is 2.6 km from the ranger station.
4

Real-World Midpoint Applications

The midpoint formula finds anything that must sit exactly halfway between two places: a meeting spot, a rest stop, the centre of a road or a pipeline. The difference in coordinates from one place to the midpoint tells how far east or west (the x’s) and how far north or south (the y’s) the halfway point is.

On a map grid, right is east and left is west; up is north and down is south. A positive change in x means east, a negative one means west. A positive change in y means north, a negative one means south.
Example 4

A shared bus stop

Two schools, Meridian at M_1(-6,\ 10) and Sunview at M_2(18,\ -4), sit on opposite sides of town. The board wants a shared bus stop exactly halfway between them.

(a) Determine the coordinates of the bus stop.
M\left(\dfrac{-6+18}{2},\ \dfrac{10+(-4)}{2}\right)
=\left(\dfrac{12}{2},\ \dfrac{6}{2}\right)=\boldsymbol{(6,\ 3)}
(b) If each grid unit represents 100 m, how far east or west, and how far north or south, is the bus stop from Meridian Elementary?
From (-6,\ 10) to (6,\ 3):
\Delta x=6-(-6)=12, \ \Delta y=3-10=-7
12\times100=\boldsymbol{1\,200} m east
7\times100=\boldsymbol{700} m south
Think Is the bus stop the same distance from both schools? How do you know without the distance formula?
Yes. From Sunview (18,\ -4) to the stop (6,\ 3) is 12 units west and 7 units north: the same 12 and 7, just in the opposite directions. That is what “halfway” means.
!
\Delta means “change in”. \Delta x is new minus old: x_{\text{stop}}-x_{\text{Meridian}}. The sign gives the direction (-7: south); the size gives the distance (7 units, so 700 m). A distance is never negative.
5

A Combined Application

Some problems need both tools, one after the other: run the midpoint formula in reverse to find the missing endpoint, then use the distance formula on the result. The key words tell you which tool each question needs.

Two tools in a row: the zip-line

First find where the cable ends (go the same again). Then measure the whole cable (run, rise, Pythagoras), and use the map scale last.

Example 5

The adventure-park zip-line

An adventure park is designing a zip-line. The starting platform is at A(2,\ 3), and the safety inspector marks the halfway cable anchor at M(10,\ 9). Each grid unit represents 40 m.

(a) Determine the coordinates of the ending platform, B, using the midpoint formula in reverse.
x_B=2(10)-2=20-2=\boldsymbol{18}
y_B=2(9)-3=18-3=\boldsymbol{15}
\boldsymbol{B(18,\ 15)}
(b) Determine the total length of the zip-line, from A to B, in metres.
d=\sqrt{(18-2)^2+(15-3)^2}=\sqrt{16^2+12^2}
=\sqrt{256+144}=\sqrt{400}=20 grid units
20\times40=\boldsymbol{800} m of zip-line cable.
Check (b) on the calculator. √(16x²+12x²)ENTER → 20, then ×40ENTER → 800.
✓

Key ideas

Pick the tool. “How far?” → distance d=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2} (subtract). “Where is halfway?” → midpoint M\left(\frac{x_1+x_2}{2},\ \frac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right) (add and halve).
Missing endpoint: double the midpoint, subtract the known endpoint: x_2=2x_M-x_1, y_2=2y_M-y_1. The middle is halfway, so go the same again. Check by finding the midpoint of your answer.
Map scale: grid units first, then multiply by the scale (13 units × 200 m = 2 600 m). Divide by 1 000 for km. East/west comes from \Delta x, north/south from \Delta y; the sign is the direction.
Both tools in a row: find the missing end with the midpoint in reverse, then the length with the distance formula, then the scale. Zip-line: B(18,\ 15), 20 units, 800 m.
