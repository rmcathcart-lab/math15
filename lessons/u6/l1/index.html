Review: The Pythagorean Theorem

A right triangle has a square corner. The two sides that make the corner are the legs, and the long side across from it is the hypotenuse. The Pythagorean theorem, a^2+b^2=c^2, connects the three sides: know any two, and you can find the third. This whole unit leans on it, because every slanted segment on a grid hides a right triangle.

I can…
  • name the two legs and the hypotenuse of a right triangle
  • state the Pythagorean theorem, a^2+b^2=c^2, and check it with numbers
  • find a missing hypotenuse: square the legs, add, then square root
  • find a missing leg: subtract the leg’s square from the hypotenuse’s square, then square root
  • use the theorem in real situations, like a ladder against a wall or a path across a field

Warm-up

Will It Fit?

Too long to lie straight? Try it corner to corner: the diagonal is longer. Each corner below is a right angle.

1
Is each diagonal a leg or the hypotenuse? How can you tell?
The hypotenuse: it is across from the right angle, and it is the longest side. The length and the width are the two legs.
2
Box ①: does the 95 cm umbrella fit lying flat, corner to corner?
Yes. 60^2+80^2=3600+6400=10\,000 and \sqrt{10\,000}=100: the diagonal is 100 cm, 5 cm to spare.
3
Drawer ②: how wide is the drawer?
25 cm. The stick is the hypotenuse and the width is a leg, so subtract: 65^2-60^2=4225-3600=625, and \sqrt{625}=25.
!
The big one. Trunk ③ is 120 cm by 50 cm. What is the longest thin rod that can lie flat on its floor? Guess first.
A 120 cm
B 130 cm
C 170 cm
B, 130 cm. 120^2+50^2=14\,400+2500=16\,900, and \sqrt{16\,900}=130.
C adds the sides (120+50), but that is walking around the edges, not cutting across. A lies along the length: the diagonal buys just 10 cm more.

1

Right Triangles and Their Parts

A right triangle has one 90^\circ angle, marked with a small square. The two sides that meet to make that angle are the legs. The third side, the one opposite the right angle, is the hypotenuse. It is always the longest side. A right triangle has two legs (in either order) but only ever one hypotenuse.
Example 1

Name the sides

Name the two legs and the hypotenuse of each right triangle.

(a) Triangle A
Legs: 5 and 12
Hypotenuse: 13
(b) Triangle B
Legs: 8 and 15
Hypotenuse: 17
(c) Triangle C
Legs: 7 and 24
Hypotenuse: 25
Think Is the hypotenuse always the slanted side at the top?
No. It is the side across from the square corner, wherever that is. Turn the triangle and the hypotenuse can be flat along the bottom (try the “roof” chip above).
2

The Pythagorean Theorem

Square the length of each leg and add the results: the total equals the square of the hypotenuse.

Pythagorean Theorem. In a right triangle with legs a and b and hypotenuse c,
\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{a}^2+\textcolor{#c27a0e}{b}^2=\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{c}^2
The letter c must be the hypotenuse: the side opposite the right angle, and the longest of the three. So c always sits alone on its own side of the equation.

Why is this true? Build a square on each side of a 3–4–5 right triangle. The areas of the two smaller squares add up exactly to the area of the largest square, and the same pattern holds for every right triangle, no matter its size.

Why does it work for every right triangle?

Here is a proof you can see. Four copies of the same right triangle sit inside one big square. Slide them around: the empty space never changes size.

Example 2

Verify the theorem

Verify the theorem by evaluating a^2+b^2 and c^2 separately, then checking they are equal.

(a) Legs 3 and 4, hypotenuse 5.
a^2+b^2=3^2+4^2=9+16=\boldsymbol{25}
c^2=5^2=\boldsymbol{25}
Both sides give 25, so the theorem holds. ✓
(b) Legs 6 and 8, hypotenuse 10.
a^2+b^2=6^2+8^2=36+64=\boldsymbol{100}
c^2=10^2=\boldsymbol{100}
Both sides give 100, so the theorem holds. ✓

It works the other way too: if the two shorter sides give a^2+b^2\neq c^2, the triangle is not a right triangle.

3

Finding the Hypotenuse

When both legs are known, solve for c by square-rooting both sides:
c=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}
Add the two squares first, then take the square root of the sum. If that sum is not a perfect square, simplify the radical as in Unit 1: \sqrt{72}=\sqrt{36\times2}=6\sqrt2.
!
Don’t add the legs. \sqrt{9^2+12^2} is not 9+12. Walking along both legs is the long way round; the hypotenuse is the shortcut. It is always longer than each leg but shorter than the two legs together.
Example 3

Find the hypotenuse

Find the length of the hypotenuse in each right triangle.

(a) Legs 9 and 12.
c^2=a^2+b^2=9^2+12^2=81+144=225
c=\sqrt{225}=\boldsymbol{15}
(b) Legs 4 and 8. Give the exact answer as a simplified mixed radical.
c^2=4^2+8^2=16+64=80
c=\sqrt{80}=\sqrt{16\times5}=\boldsymbol{4\sqrt5}
Check: 4\sqrt5\approx8.94, longer than both legs. ✓
Square roots. \sqrt{\phantom{x}} is 2ndx², and it opens a bracket. All in one go: √9x²+12x²)ENTER gives 15. Check that \sqrt{80} and 4\sqrt5 give the same decimal.

A Unit 1 refresher: simplifying a square root

Look for the biggest perfect square that divides the number under the root, and take its square root out front. If no perfect square (other than 1) divides it, the root is already as simple as it gets.

4

Finding a Missing Leg

If the hypotenuse and one leg are known, subtract instead of adding:
a=\sqrt{c^2-b^2}\qquad\text{or}\qquad b=\sqrt{c^2-a^2}
Always subtract the leg’s square from the hypotenuse’s square, never the reverse: the hypotenuse is the longest side.
!
Big square minus small square. 5^2-13^2=25-169=-144, and a negative number has no square root: the calculator says DOMAIN error. If that happens, you subtracted the wrong way round.
Subtract inside the root. √13x²−5x²)ENTER gives 12. Try it the wrong way round to see the error.
Example 4

Find the missing leg

Find the length of the missing leg in each right triangle.

(a) Hypotenuse 13, one leg 5.
b^2=c^2-a^2=13^2-5^2=169-25=144
b=\sqrt{144}=\boldsymbol{12}
(b) Hypotenuse 25, one leg 7.
b^2=c^2-a^2=25^2-7^2=625-49=576
b=\sqrt{576}=\boldsymbol{24}
5

Real-World Applications

The theorem works anywhere a right angle appears: a ladder against a wall, the diagonal of a rectangular field, a screen size. The first step never changes: find the right angle, decide whether the unknown side is the hypotenuse or a leg, then use the matching form of the theorem.

1Find the right angle (the square corner).
2Is the unknown across from it? Then it’s the hypotenuse: add.
3Does the unknown touch the corner? Then it’s a leg: subtract.
Example 5

Ladders and fields

Draw on the diagram, then solve.

(a) A 17 ft ladder leans against a vertical wall. Its base sits 8 ft from the wall. How high up the wall does the ladder reach?
The ladder is the hypotenuse; h is a leg.
h^2=17^2-8^2=289-64=225
h=\sqrt{225}=\boldsymbol{15} ft up the wall.
(b) A rectangular field is 6 m wide and 8 m long. A path is worn diagonally across it, corner to corner. Find the length of the path.
The diagonal is the hypotenuse of a 6–8 right triangle.
d^2=6^2+8^2=36+64=100
d=\sqrt{100}=\boldsymbol{10} m long.

The sliding ladder: the hypotenuse never changes

Pull the foot of the ladder away from the wall and the top slides down. The ladder stays 17 ft long, so it is always the hypotenuse.

Math Historyc. 1800 BCE · Babylon (now Iraq)

A table of right triangles, 3 800 years old

Plimpton 322 is a clay tablet from ancient Babylon, written around 1800 BCE, over 1000 years before Pythagoras was born. Today it is at Columbia University in New York.

Its 15 rows each give the short side and the diagonal of a right triangle, in whole numbers, written in base 60. Row 1 reads “1 59” and “2 49”: 1\times60+59=119 and 2\times60+49=169.

Find the other leg of the Row 1 triangle (short side 119, hypotenuse 169).
169^2-119^2=28\,561-14\,161=14\,400, and \sqrt{14\,400}=\boldsymbol{120}. A whole number!
✓

Key ideas

The parts. The two sides that make the square corner are the legs. The side across from the corner is the hypotenuse, always the longest side.
The theorem. a^2+b^2=c^2: the squares on the legs add up to the square on the hypotenuse. Only c, the hypotenuse, sits alone.
Missing hypotenuse: square, add, then square root: c=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}. Missing leg: square, subtract (big minus small), then square root: b=\sqrt{c^2-a^2}.
Word problems: find the right angle first, decide hypotenuse or leg, then add or subtract. Give exact answers (4\sqrt5) or decimals (\approx8.94) as asked, with units.
