The Midpoint Formula

Last lesson asked how far apart two points are. Today asks a different question: where is the point exactly halfway between them? That point is the midpoint. Count to it, walk to it, or use the shortcut: average the coordinates.

I can…
  • explain what the midpoint of a line segment is
  • find a midpoint on a grid by counting squares, or by walking half the run and half the rise
  • use the midpoint formula M\left(\frac{x_1+x_2}{2},\ \frac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right), with positive and negative coordinates

Meet Me Halfway

Friends always meet exactly halfway between their two houses. 1 square = 1 block.

Pick two friends:
1
Ava and Ben live on the same street. Where do they meet?
(-2,\ 3). Same street, so y stays 3. They are 6 blocks apart, so each walks 3.
2
Ben and Cara meet halfway. What is there?
The Bakery, (3,\ 0). Ben to Cara is 4 right and 6 down; halfway is 2 right and 3 down.

Halfway between 2 and 8 is ___. What’s a shortcut?

Which two friends meet at Town Hall?
Ava and Cara: (-5+5)\div2=0 and (3+(-3))\div2=0.

1

What Is a Midpoint?

The midpoint M of segment PQ is the one point exactly halfway between the endpoints. So PM=MQ: M splits the segment into two equal pieces.

The distance formula subtracts coordinates to measure how far apart two points are. The midpoint asks a different question: where is the point in the middle? On a number line, the halfway number is found by adding and halving.

2

Locating a Midpoint by Counting

On a grid, the midpoint of a horizontal or vertical segment can be found just by counting squares: count the squares between the endpoints, and step in half that many. For a diagonal segment, walk half the run across and half the rise up (or down).

Example 1

Count to the middle

Count squares to locate and label the midpoint M, then record the three points.

(a) Horizontal segment AB. Describe how the x-coordinates of A and B combine to give the x-coordinate of M.
A(-5,\ -2) B(3,\ -2) M(-1,\ -2)
AB spans 8 squares, so step 4 right from A: \boldsymbol{M(-1,\ -2)}.
The x-coordinates are averaged: \dfrac{-5+3}{2}=\dfrac{-2}{2}=-1. The y-coordinate stays -2: both endpoints sit on y=-2.
(b) Vertical segment CD. Describe how the y-coordinates of C and D combine to give the y-coordinate of M.
C(4,\ 5) D(4,\ -3) M(4,\ 1)
CD spans 8 squares, so step 4 down from C: \boldsymbol{M(4,\ 1)}.
The y-coordinates are averaged: \dfrac{5+(-3)}{2}=\dfrac{2}{2}=1. The x-coordinate stays 4: both endpoints sit on x=4.

A diagonal segment: half the run, half the rise

A diagonal segment has no row of squares to count along. Walk the triangle instead: go across and up or down, but stop after half of each. You are standing on the midpoint.

Example 2

An oblique segment

The oblique segment EF below has E(1,\ 1) and F(5,\ 5).

Walk half the run and half the rise from E to locate the midpoint M, and label it on the grid. Record the coordinates of E, F, and M.
E(1,\ 1) F(5,\ 5) M(3,\ 3)
From E(1,\ 1) to F(5,\ 5) is 4 right and 4 up. Half of that is 2 right and 2 up, which lands on \boldsymbol{M(3,\ 3)}.
Check: does averaging the x-coordinates of E and F give the x-coordinate you found for M? Does averaging the y-coordinates give its y-coordinate?
\dfrac{1+5}{2}=\dfrac{6}{2}=3 and \dfrac{1+5}{2}=3. Yes: averaging each coordinate gives the same point as counting.
3

The Midpoint Formula

The counting pattern becomes a formula. With the formula there is no need for a grid at all.

The Midpoint Formula. For P(x_1,\ y_1) and Q(x_2,\ y_2), the midpoint is
M\left(\dfrac{\textcolor{#c27a0e}{x_1+x_2}}{2},\ \dfrac{\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{y_1+y_2}}{2}\right)
Average the x’s, average the y’s.
!
Add, don’t subtract. The distance formula subtracts coordinates (how far apart?). The midpoint formula adds them and halves (where is the middle?). And don’t forget the \div 2!

Try it: drag the endpoints

Drag P or Q to any grid point. The midpoint always lands halfway, and its coordinates are always the averages. (Tap a point to remove it; tap the grid to put one back.)

Example 3

Midpoints from the formula

Determine the coordinates of the midpoint of the segment with each pair of endpoints.

(a) P(2,\ 8),\ Q(10,\ 4)
M=\left(\dfrac{2+10}{2},\ \dfrac{8+4}{2}\right)=\left(\dfrac{12}{2},\ \dfrac{12}{2}\right)
M=\boldsymbol{(6,\ 6)}
(b) R(5,\ 1),\ S(11,\ 9)
M=\left(\dfrac{5+11}{2},\ \dfrac{1+9}{2}\right)=\left(\dfrac{16}{2},\ \dfrac{10}{2}\right)
M=\boldsymbol{(8,\ 5)}
Example 4

Negative coordinates

Same task with negative coordinates. The formula is unchanged: add the signed numbers carefully.

(a) A(-6,\ 3),\ B(2,\ -7)
M=\left(\dfrac{-6+2}{2},\ \dfrac{3+(-7)}{2}\right)=\left(\dfrac{-4}{2},\ \dfrac{-4}{2}\right)
M=\boldsymbol{(-2,\ -2)}
(b) C(-9,\ -4),\ D(3,\ -10)
M=\left(\dfrac{-9+3}{2},\ \dfrac{-4+(-10)}{2}\right)=\left(\dfrac{-6}{2},\ \dfrac{-14}{2}\right)
M=\boldsymbol{(-3,\ -7)}
(c) P(-3,\ 4),\ Q(2,\ -1) (A midpoint does not have to land on a grid point.)
M=\left(\dfrac{-3+2}{2},\ \dfrac{4+(-1)}{2}\right)=\left(\dfrac{-1}{2},\ \dfrac{3}{2}\right)
M=\boldsymbol{(-0.5,\ 1.5)}
Brackets around the sum. Add first, then halve: ((−)4+(−)10)÷2ENTER gives −7. Without the brackets the calculator divides first: -4+(-10\div2)=-9. Use (−) for a negative number, not the minus key.

Spot the mistake

Four students found the same midpoint. Only one is right. Plot each answer: the real midpoint sits on the segment, exactly halfway.

4

Midpoints on a Map

On a coordinate map, “exactly halfway between” two places means the midpoint of the segment joining them. Substitute the two locations into the formula, just like any other pair of points.

Example 5

A rest area halfway

A trail committee lays a coordinate grid over a map, with each unit representing 1 km. Fairview is at (-15,\ 22) and Oakdale is at (35,\ -8). The committee wants a rest area exactly halfway between the two towns. Determine its coordinates.

M=\left(\dfrac{-15+35}{2},\ \dfrac{22+(-8)}{2}\right)=\left(\dfrac{20}{2},\ \dfrac{14}{2}\right)=\boldsymbol{(10,\ 7)}
The rest area belongs at (10,\ 7): 10 km east and 7 km north of the map’s origin.
Think How could you check that (10,\ 7) really is halfway?
Walk from Fairview to (10,\ 7): 25 east and 15 south. Then from (10,\ 7) to Oakdale: 25 east and 15 south again. The same trip twice, so the two halves are equal (each is \sqrt{25^2+15^2}\approx29.2 km).
✓

Key ideas

The midpoint is the point exactly halfway between the endpoints: it splits the segment into two equal pieces, PM=MQ.
On a grid: count the squares and step in half. On a diagonal, walk half the run and half the rise.
The formula: M\left(\frac{x_1+x_2}{2},\ \frac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right). A midpoint is an average: add, then halve. Watch the signs with negatives.
Add, don’t subtract. Distance subtracts (how far?). Midpoint adds and halves (where?). A midpoint can land between grid lines, like (-1.5,\ 0).
