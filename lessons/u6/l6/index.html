Rise Over Run: The Slope of a Line Segment

How steep is it? The tallest slide is not always the scariest. Steepness depends on how the climb up (the rise) compares with the distance across (the run). That comparison, rise over run, is called the slope.

I can…
  • explain that slope measures how steep a line is
  • count the rise (up) and the run (right) between two points on a grid
  • write the slope as a fraction \dfrac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}, in lowest terms
  • use the slope formula m=\dfrac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1} when I only have the coordinates, even with negatives
  • find the slope of a ramp, road or trail from its climb and its distance across
RF3 Demonstrate an understanding of slope with respect to: rise and run; line segments and lines; rate of change; parallel lines; perpendicular lines.
Today: the rise and the run from the last lessons, now compared with each other. That is slope.

1

What Slope Measures

Every step of a staircase goes up a little and across a little. A straight edge laid along the front corners is a line. How steep it is depends on how the “up” compares with the “across”.

The rise is the vertical change between two points. The run is the horizontal change. The slope m compares them:
m=\dfrac{\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{\text{rise}}}{\textcolor{#c27a0e}{\text{run}}}=\dfrac{\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{y_2-y_1}}{\textcolor{#c27a0e}{x_2-x_1}}

Rise is positive counting up, negative counting down. Run is positive counting right, negative counting left. Every segment in this lesson climbs to the right, so every slope here is positive.

The warm-up again: tallest isn’t steepest

Put all five slides at the same starting point. The steepest one is the one with the biggest \dfrac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}, not the tallest one.

2

Counting Rise and Run from a Graph

With both points plotted, just count squares: how many up from one point to the other’s height, then how many right to the other’s position. Don’t mix the two up: rise goes on top, run on the bottom.

Example 1

Count the rise, count the run

Points A and B are plotted on the grid below.

(a) Starting at A, count how many squares up you must go to reach the same height as B. This is the rise.
A(1,\ 1) and B(5,\ 4): count up from y=1 to y=4.
rise =4-1=\boldsymbol{3} squares up
(b) Starting at A, count how many squares right you must go to reach the same horizontal position as B. This is the run.
Count right from x=1 to x=5.
run =5-1=\boldsymbol{4} squares right
(c) Use (a) and (b) to write the slope of \overline{AB} as a fraction, \dfrac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}.
m=\dfrac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{3}{4}}
The segment rises 3 squares for every 4 squares to the right.
Example 2

Two segments, two slopes

Count rise and run for each segment, then write its slope.

SegmentRiseRunSlope
CD33\dfrac{3}{3}=\boldsymbol{1}
EF51\dfrac{5}{1}=\boldsymbol{5}
CD: from C(0,\ 1) to D(3,\ 4), up 3 and right 3: slope \boldsymbol{1}.
EF: from E(1,\ 0) to F(2,\ 5), up 5 and right 1: slope \boldsymbol{5}.
EF is much steeper, and its slope is much bigger.

Rise on top, run on the bottom

The order matters. \dfrac{3}{4} and \dfrac{4}{3} describe two very different lines: flip the fraction and you get a much steeper line that misses the point you were aiming for.

3

The Slope Formula

When only the coordinates are given, the counting is done by subtraction. For P(x_1,\ y_1) and Q(x_2,\ y_2):

m=\dfrac{\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{y_2-y_1}}{\textcolor{#c27a0e}{x_2-x_1}}\qquad\begin{aligned}&\leftarrow\ \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{\text{rise}}\\ &\leftarrow\ \textcolor{#c27a0e}{\text{run}}\end{aligned}
Subtract in the same order top and bottom: whichever point you call (x_1,\ y_1), its coordinates are the ones you subtract, top and bottom. Reduce the fraction at the end.

Lowest terms: smaller triangles, same slope

Reducing the fraction is not just a rule: a big slope triangle splits into smaller copies, and each copy has the same slope. \dfrac{6}{3}=\dfrac{2}{1} means “2 up for every 1 right”.

Lowest terms on the calculator. The a b/c key builds a fraction and ENTER reduces it: 4a b/c6ENTER shows 2┘3, which means \tfrac{2}{3}.
Example 3

The slope formula

Determine the slope of the line segment joining P(1,\ 2) and Q(4,\ 8).

Find m.
Let P(1,\ 2)=(x_1,\ y_1) and Q(4,\ 8)=(x_2,\ y_2).
m=\dfrac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}=\dfrac{8-2}{4-1}=\dfrac{6}{3}=\boldsymbol{2}
Rise 6, run 3: the segment climbs 2 units for every 1 unit to the right.
Check by counting on the grid: up 6, right 3 ✓
Example 4

With negative coordinates

Determine the slope of the segment joining R(-3,\ -4) and S(3,\ 0). The negatives change nothing about the method: just track the signs, then simplify the fraction.

Find m.
Let R(-3,\ -4)=(x_1,\ y_1) and S(3,\ 0)=(x_2,\ y_2).
m=\dfrac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}=\dfrac{0-(-4)}{3-(-3)}=\dfrac{4}{6}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{2}{3}}
Check by counting: up 4, right 6, and \tfrac{4}{6}=\tfrac{2}{3} ✓
!
Brackets around a negative. 0-(-4)=0+4=4 and 3-(-3)=3+3=6. Subtracting a negative is adding. A segment that climbs to the right always gets a positive rise and a positive run.
4

Slope in the Real World

Ramps, roads and trails are described by the same ratio: vertical climb over horizontal distance. Read the rise and the run off the description, then reduce.

Grade. Road signs and trail maps often give the slope as a percent, called the grade: divide, then multiply by 100. A slope of \tfrac{1}{10}=0.10 is a 10% grade: it climbs 10 m for every 100 m across.
Example 5

The hiking trail

A hiking trail climbs from the trailhead at (0,\ 0) to a lookout at (300,\ 45), each unit 1 m. Determine the slope of the trail as a fraction in lowest terms.

Find m.
m=\dfrac{45-0}{300-0}=\dfrac{45}{300}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{3}{20}}, a 15% grade: the trail climbs 3 m for every 20 m travelled horizontally.
✓

Key ideas

Slope measures steepness. It compares the rise (vertical change) with the run (horizontal change): m=\frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}. Tallest isn’t steepest: compare up with across.
On a grid: count squares up to the other point’s height (rise), then right to it (run). Rise on top, run on the bottom: \frac{3}{4} and \frac{4}{3} are different lines.
Slope formula: m=\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}. Same order top and bottom; brackets around negatives: \frac{0-(-4)}{3-(-3)}=\frac{4}{6}.
Lowest terms: \frac{4}{6}=\frac{2}{3} means “2 up for every 3 right”. Real world: \frac{45}{300}=\frac{3}{20}, a 15% grade.
