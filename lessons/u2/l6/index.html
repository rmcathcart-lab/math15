Negative Exponents

Last lesson the pattern took us down to a^0=1. Today we keep going, past zero: 2^{-1}, 2^{-2}, 2^{-3}… A negative exponent does not make a number negative. It means take the reciprocal: 2^{-3}=\dfrac{1}{2^3}=\dfrac18.

I can…
  • continue the pattern of powers past a^0=1 and explain what a negative exponent means
  • use the rule a^{-n}=\dfrac{1}{a^n} to write a power with a positive exponent, then evaluate it
  • flip a negative exponent up from the denominator: \dfrac{1}{a^{-n}}=a^n
  • move only the base the exponent belongs to: 6x^{-3}=\dfrac{6}{x^3}
  • finish a quotient-law answer like x^{-3} by writing it with a positive exponent

Today: negative exponents.
1

Extending the Pattern Past Zero

From Lesson 5, a^0=1 for any nonzero base. Watch powers of 2 fall as the exponent drops by one: each one is the one before it divided by 2. Then keep dividing!

2^3=8 
2^2=48\div2
2^1=24\div2
2^0=12\div2
2^{-1}=\dfrac12=\dfrac{1}{2^1}1\div2
2^{-2}=\dfrac14=\dfrac{1}{2^2}\tfrac12\div2

A negative exponent gives the reciprocal (one over) of the matching positive power: 2^{-2}=\dfrac{1}{2^{2}}.

2

The Negative Exponent Rule

Negative Exponent Rule  For any base a\neq0,
a^{-n}=\dfrac{1}{a^{n}}
In words: take the reciprocal and make the exponent positive.

A negative exponent makes a number small, not negative

!
2^{-3}=\dfrac{1}{2^3}=\dfrac18. It is not -8, and it is not -6. With a whole-number base bigger than 1, the answer is always a positive fraction less than 1.
With a negative base, brackets matter just as in Lesson 1: (-2)^{-2}=\dfrac{1}{(-2)^{2}}=\dfrac14, but -2^{-2}=-\dfrac{1}{2^{2}}=-\dfrac14.
Check on the calculator. For a negative exponent use the negative key (−), not the subtract key: 2^(−)3ENTER gives 0.125. Then 2ndF◂▸D turns it into 1┘8, which means \tfrac18.
Example 1

Positive exponent first, then evaluate

Write each power with a positive exponent, then evaluate.

(a) 3^{-2}
\dfrac{1}{3^{2}}=\mathbf{\dfrac{1}{9}}
(b) 4^{-1}
\dfrac{1}{4^{1}}=\mathbf{\dfrac{1}{4}}
(c) 5^{-3}
\dfrac{1}{5^{3}}=\mathbf{\dfrac{1}{125}}
(d) 10^{-2}
\dfrac{1}{10^{2}}=\dfrac{1}{100}
=\mathbf{0.01}
Think Which is bigger, 2^{-3} or 2^{-4}? Is either one negative?
2^{-3}=\tfrac18 is bigger than 2^{-4}=\tfrac1{16}: one more step down the pattern halves it again. Neither is negative: both are small positive numbers.
3

Variable Bases

When the base is a variable, there is no final number to work out. Just apply the rule and stop: x^{-3}=\dfrac{1}{x^{3}}. It works the same with a letter for the exponent too: a^{-n}=\dfrac{1}{a^{n}}.

Example 2

Variable bases

Write each expression with a positive exponent.

(a) x^{-3}
\mathbf{\dfrac{1}{x^{3}}}
(b) y^{-5}
\mathbf{\dfrac{1}{y^{5}}}
(c) m^{-1}
\mathbf{\dfrac{1}{m}}
(d) a^{-n}
\mathbf{\dfrac{1}{a^{n}}}
4

Negative Exponents in the Denominator

If the negative exponent is already in a denominator, use the rule twice: \dfrac{1}{a^{-n}} is 1 divided by \dfrac{1}{a^{n}}, which is a^{n}.

Reverse Rule  For any base a\neq0,
\dfrac{1}{a^{-n}}=a^{n}
A negative exponent downstairs flips up to the numerator and turns positive.
Example 3

Flip it up from the denominator

Simplify each expression so the exponent is positive, then evaluate where possible.

(a) \dfrac{1}{2^{-3}}
=2^{3}
=\mathbf{8}
(b) \dfrac{1}{3^{-2}}
=3^{2}
=\mathbf{9}
(c) \dfrac{1}{x^{-4}}
=\mathbf{x^{4}}
(d) \dfrac{1}{y^{-6}}
=\mathbf{y^{6}}

5

Coefficients

A negative exponent belongs only to the base written right beside it, never to the coefficient out front. In 6x^{-3} the exponent -3 belongs to x alone, so 6x^{-3}=6\times\dfrac{1}{x^{3}}=\dfrac{6}{x^{3}}. The 6 stays upstairs.

Not the same:  6x^{-3}\neq\dfrac{1}{6x^{3}}. Only the factor the exponent touches moves across the fraction bar. Everything else stays where it is.
Example 4

The coefficient stays put

Simplify each expression, writing your answer with a positive exponent.

(a) 6x^{-3}
6\times\dfrac{1}{x^{3}}=\mathbf{\dfrac{6}{x^{3}}}
The 6 does not move.
(b) 2a^{-1}
2\times\dfrac{1}{a}=\mathbf{\dfrac{2}{a}}
(c) \dfrac{9}{y^{-2}}
9\times y^{2}
=\mathbf{9y^{2}}
(d) \dfrac{8}{b^{-4}}
8\times b^{4}
=\mathbf{8b^{4}}
6

Negative Exponents from the Quotient Law

You will often meet a negative exponent as the answer to a quotient-law question: whenever the exponent you subtract is the bigger one. Subtract as usual, then finish by rewriting the answer with a positive exponent.

Example 5

Subtract, then make it positive

Simplify using the quotient law, then rewrite the result with a positive exponent.

(a) x^{2}\div x^{5}
x^{2-5}=x^{-3}
=\mathbf{\dfrac{1}{x^{3}}}
(b) m^{3}\div m^{8}
m^{3-8}=m^{-5}
=\mathbf{\dfrac{1}{m^{5}}}
(c) \dfrac{12x^{2}}{3x^{6}}
(12\div3)x^{2-6}
=4x^{-4}=\mathbf{\dfrac{4}{x^{4}}}
(d) \dfrac{20x^{5}y^{2}}{4x^{2}y^{6}}
(20\div4)x^{5-2}y^{2-6}
=5x^{3}y^{-4}=\mathbf{\dfrac{5x^{3}}{y^{4}}}

The Fridge Mould

A super-mould on a forgotten sandwich triples every hour. At noon it is 1 cm². Tap a ? to reveal it.

1
How big is the spot at 4 pm?
81 cm²: triple 27.
2
At what time does it reach 243 cm²?
5 pm: 81 × 3 = 243.
3
Run the clock backwards. How big was it at 11 am? At 10 am?
Going back an hour undoes a tripling: divide by 3. 11 am: 1\div3=\tfrac13 cm²; 10 am: \tfrac13\div3=\tfrac19 cm².
!
The big one. Nobody saw any mould at midnight, 12 hours before noon. If it had been tripling all night, how big was the spot at midnight? Guess first (in cm²).
A -36
B 0
C \dfrac{1}{531\,441}
C. Twelve hours back means dividing by 3 twelve times: 1\div3^{12}=\tfrac{1}{531\,441} cm². As a square, its side is \tfrac{1}{729} cm, about 0.014 mm: much thinner than a hair. That’s why nobody saw it!
A comes from “3\times(-12)”: a size can’t be negative. B: dividing by 3 never reaches 0.

Argue it with a neighbour: going back in time, does the spot ever shrink to exactly zero?

✓

Key ideas

The pattern keeps going past 0: divide by the base each step. 2^0=1, 2^{-1}=\tfrac12, 2^{-2}=\tfrac14.
The rule: a^{-n}=\dfrac{1}{a^{n}}: take the reciprocal, make the exponent positive. 2^{-3}=\tfrac18: small, not negative.
Downstairs flips up: \dfrac{1}{a^{-n}}=a^{n}. Move only what the exponent touches: 6x^{-3}=\dfrac{6}{x^{3}}.
Quotient law first, then positive: x^2\div x^5=x^{-3}=\dfrac{1}{x^3}.
