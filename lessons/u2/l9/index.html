Rational Exponents and Radical Form

What could 25^{1/2} or 8^{2/3} mean? We can’t use 25 “half a time” as a factor. But if the exponent laws still work for fractions, there is only one answer: a fraction exponent is a root. Today we work out why, evaluate powers like these without a calculator, switch between powers and radicals, and use the laws with fraction exponents.

I can…
  • explain, with the product law, why 25^{1/2}=\sqrt{25}: the denominator names the root
  • evaluate powers like 64^{1/3} and 27^{2/3} without a calculator, taking the root first
  • write a power like y^{2/3} as a radical, and a radical like \sqrt[4]{y^3} as a power
  • use the exponent laws with fraction exponents: add, subtract or multiply the fractions, then simplify

Unit Fractional Exponents: What Does a^{1/n} Mean?

The product law should keep working for fractions. Then 5^{1/2}\times5^{1/2}=5^{\frac12+\frac12}=5^{1}=5. That is exactly what \sqrt5\times\sqrt5 does, so 5^{1/2}=\sqrt5. Likewise 3^{1/3}\times3^{1/3}\times3^{1/3}=3, so 3^{1/3}=\sqrt[3]{3}.

A unit fraction exponent is a root (for a\ge0)
a^{1/\textcolor{#13807a}{n}}=\sqrt[\textcolor{#13807a}{n}]{a}
The denominator names the root: \tfrac12 is a square root, \tfrac13 a cube root, \tfrac14 a fourth root.
Handy powers to know (no calculator on the test!)
squares4,\ 9,\ 16,\ 25,\ 36,\ 49,\ 64,\ 81,\ 100,\ 121,\ 144
cubes8,\ 27,\ 64,\ 125,\ 1\,000
4th powers16,\ 81,\ 256,\ 625

Evaluating a^{1/n}: which number, used n times, gives a?

To find 64^{1/3}=\sqrt[3]{64}, ask: which number, used 3 times as a factor, gives 64? Try 1, 2, 3, … until it fits.

General Rational Exponents: a^{m/n}

The numerator is just an ordinary power. The denominator is still the root. Think “power over root”.

numerator m
the power
a^{\textcolor{#c27a0e}{m}/\textcolor{#13807a}{n}}=\left(\sqrt[\textcolor{#13807a}{n}]{a}\right)^{\textcolor{#c27a0e}{m}}=\sqrt[\textcolor{#13807a}{n}]{a^{\textcolor{#c27a0e}{m}}}
denominator n
the root
Like a plant: the root is at the bottom, the power grows on top.

The power can come before or after the root. Both orders agree: 8^{2/3}=\left(\sqrt[3]{8}\right)^{2}=2^{2}=4 and 8^{2/3}=\sqrt[3]{8^{2}}=\sqrt[3]{64}=4. But rooting first keeps the numbers small, so take that path whenever the base has a nice root.

Checking at home? Brackets around the fraction. 9^(3÷2)ENTER gives 27. Without the brackets the calculator does 9^3\div2=364.5. (The unit test is no-calculator: root first by hand!)

Converting Between Radical Form and Rational Exponent Form

The same match-up works for a variable base, in both directions: x^{m/n}=\left(\sqrt[n]{x}\right)^{m}=\sqrt[n]{x^{m}}, and any radical \sqrt[n]{x^{m}} can be rewritten as x^{m/n}. The index becomes the denominator and the inside power becomes the numerator. A negative exponent still means “take the reciprocal”, just as in Lesson 6.

Radical → power

Math History1676

Isaac Newton writes it down

For a long time, roots were written only with root signs. In June 1676, Isaac Newton sent a letter (meant for the German mathematician Leibniz) explaining his new notation: since people write a^2 and a^3 for aa and aaa, he would write a^{1/2}, a^{3/2} and a^{5/3} for \sqrt{a}, \sqrt{a^3} and the cube root of a^5.

He wasn’t the first to think of fraction exponents (John Wallis used them in 1656), but this is the notation we still use: power over root.

Write Newton’s a^{5/3} as a radical, the way we would today.
Denominator 3 is the index, numerator 5 the power: a^{5/3}=\sqrt[3]{a^{5}}.

Applying Exponent Laws to Rational Exponents

Every law from this unit works unchanged on rational exponents. There is no need to change to radicals first. For the product law, add the exponents over a common denominator: a^{1/2}\times a^{1/3}=a^{\frac36+\frac26}=a^{5/6}. Change to radical form only at the very end, once the exponent is fully simplified.

Product law
a^m\times a^n=a^{m+n}
add the fractions
Quotient law
a^m\div a^n=a^{m-n}
subtract the fractions
Power of a power
(a^m)^n=a^{mn}
multiply the fractions
Power of a product
(ab)^n=a^nb^n
every factor gets the power

The fraction toolbox

With fraction exponents, each law turns into a small fraction calculation. These are the moves you need:

Add or subtract
Get a common denominator first.
\tfrac14+\tfrac12=\tfrac14+\tfrac24=\tfrac34
Multiply
Top × top, bottom × bottom. A whole number is over 1.
\tfrac13\times6=\tfrac13\times\tfrac61=\tfrac63=2
Simplify
Always reduce the exponent at the end.
c^{4/6}=c^{2/3}
Exponent 1
\tfrac22=\tfrac44=1, and p^1 is just p.
q^{\frac12+\frac12}=q^{1}=q
!
The outside power hits the number too. In a bracket like \left(4g^{1/2}\right)^{2}, the 4 is squared as well: 4^{2}=16.

✓

Key ideas

A fraction exponent is a root. The product law forces it: a^{1/2}\times a^{1/2}=a^1, so a^{1/2}=\sqrt{a}. The denominator names the root.
Power over root: a^{m/n}=\left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^m=\sqrt[n]{a^m}. Take the root first: 27^{2/3}=3^2=9.
Both ways: y^{2/3}=\sqrt[3]{y^2} and \sqrt{z^5}=z^{5/2} (index 2). Negative means reciprocal: z^{-1/2}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt z}.
Same laws, fraction arithmetic: add (common denominator), subtract, or multiply the exponents, then simplify: c^{4/6}=c^{2/3}.
