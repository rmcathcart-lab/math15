Power of a Product and Power of a Quotient

So far the brackets held a single power, like (m^{2})^{3}. Today they hold a product, like (3x)^{2}, or a fraction, like \left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{3}. The big idea: the outer exponent goes to every factor inside. Each one gets its own copy.

I can…
  • use the Power of a Product Law: (3x)^{2}=3^{2}x^{2}=9x^{2} (the number inside gets the exponent too)
  • use the Power of a Quotient Law: \left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{3}=\frac{x^{3}}{2^{3}}=\frac{x^{3}}{8} (the top and the bottom)
  • tell where an exponent reaches: 3x^{2} is not the same as (3x)^{2}
  • combine all five exponent laws, one law at a time, and show every step
AN3 Demonstrate an understanding of powers with integral and rational exponents.
Today: the last two of the five exponent laws, and using the laws together.

1

Exponent Laws You Already Know

Three laws carry over from Lessons 1 and 2. Each one is just counting factors.

Product Law
a^{m}\times a^{n}=a^{m+n}
Same base, multiplied: add the exponents.
Quotient Law
a^{m}\div a^{n}=a^{m-n}
Same base, divided: subtract the exponents.
Power of a Power Law
(a^{m})^{n}=a^{mn}
A power in brackets, raised to a power: multiply the exponents.

2

The Power of a Product Law

Write it out to see what happens to a whole product in brackets:

(xy)^{3}=(xy)(xy)(xy)=(x\,x\,x)(y\,y\,y)=x^{3}y^{3}

Each factor picks up its own copy of the exponent.

Power of a Product Law  (ab)^{n}=a^{n}b^{n}
Check with numbers: (2\times3)^{2}=6^{2}=36 and 2^{2}\times3^{2}=4\times9=36. Same answer.
!
The number gets it too. In (3x)^{2} the 3 is inside the brackets, so it is squared: 3^{2}x^{2}=9x^{2}, not 3x^{2}.
!
A negative inside. In (-4y)^{2} the whole -4 is squared: (-4)^{2}=16. Two negatives make a positive.

Back to the warm-up: supersize a tile

Let one tile have side s, so its area is s^{2}. The 2 times picture has side 2s, and its area is (2s)^{2}. The square goes to the 2 and to the s.

Where does the exponent reach?

Brackets act like a fence. The exponent repeats only what is inside the fence. With no brackets, it reaches only the one symbol right under it.

Example 2

Power of a product

Use the Power of a Product Law to simplify each expression.

(a) (3x)^{2}
3^{2}x^{2}
=\boldsymbol{9x^{2}}
(b) (2m)^{4}
2^{4}m^{4}
=\boldsymbol{16m^{4}}
(c) (-4y)^{2}
(-4)^{2}y^{2}
=\boldsymbol{16y^{2}}
(d) (2ab)^{3}
2^{3}a^{3}b^{3}
=\boldsymbol{8a^{3}b^{3}}
Think In (d), how many factors are inside the brackets? How many times is each one used?
Three factors: 2, a and b. Each is used 3 times: 2\cdot2\cdot2=8, a\cdot a\cdot a=a^{3}, b\cdot b\cdot b=b^{3}.
3

The Power of a Quotient Law

The same idea works on a fraction:

\left(\dfrac{a}{b}\right)^{3}=\dfrac{a}{b}\cdot\dfrac{a}{b}\cdot\dfrac{a}{b}=\dfrac{a\,a\,a}{b\,b\,b}=\dfrac{a^{3}}{b^{3}}

Power of a Quotient Law  \left(\dfrac{a}{b}\right)^{n}=\dfrac{a^{n}}{b^{n}},\quad b\neq0
Raise the top and the bottom to the power separately. Check: \left(\dfrac{4}{5}\right)^{2}=\dfrac{16}{25}=\dfrac{4^{2}}{5^{2}}.

Squaring a fraction: the area picture

Take a square with side 1. A square with side \frac{3}{4} covers \left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{2} of it. Count the small squares: the top counts the shaded ones, the bottom counts them all.

Example 3

Power of a quotient

Use the Power of a Quotient Law to simplify each expression.

(a) \left(\dfrac{3}{4}\right)^{2}
\dfrac{3^{2}}{4^{2}}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{9}{16}}
(b) \left(\dfrac{x}{2}\right)^{3}
\dfrac{x^{3}}{2^{3}}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{x^{3}}{8}}
(c) \left(\dfrac{5}{y}\right)^{2}
\dfrac{5^{2}}{y^{2}}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{25}{y^{2}}}
(d) \left(\dfrac{m}{n}\right)^{4}
\boldsymbol{\dfrac{m^{4}}{n^{4}}}
Think Is \left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{2} bigger or smaller than \frac{3}{4}? Why?
Smaller. \frac{9}{16} is a bit more than a half, and \frac{3}{4}=\frac{12}{16}. Taking \frac{3}{4} of \frac{3}{4} leaves less than you started with (the area picture shows it).
4

Combining the Laws

Most expressions need more than one law. Use them one at a time:

First distribute the outer exponent: the Power of a Product or Power of a Quotient Law.
Then combine any matching bases: the Product or Quotient Law.

(3x)^{2}\times x^{3}=3^{2}x^{2}\times x^{3}=9x^{2+3}=9x^{5}

Example 4

Two laws

Simplify each expression using two exponent laws.

(a) (2x)^{3}\times x^{2}
2^{3}x^{3}\times x^{2}
=8x^{3+2}=\boldsymbol{8x^{5}}
(b) (5m^{2})^{2}
5^{2}\left(m^{2}\right)^{2}
=\boldsymbol{25m^{4}}
(c) (y^{3})^{2}\div y^{4}
y^{6}\div y^{4}
=y^{6-4}=\boldsymbol{y^{2}}
(d) \left(\dfrac{a}{3}\right)^{2}\times a^{3}
\dfrac{a^{2}}{9}\times a^{3}
=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{a^{5}}{9}}
Think In (b), which two laws did you use?
Power of a Product (the 5 and the m^{2} are each squared), then Power of a Power (\left(m^{2}\right)^{2}=m^{2\times2}=m^{4}).
5

Putting It All Together

These expressions use three or more laws. Work step by step: one law at a time, and show every stage. A number inside the brackets gets the outer exponent too.

Not sure your answer is right? Check it with a number. Put the same value in for the letter in the question and in your answer. If they are really equal, you get the same number both times.

Example 5

Three or more laws

Simplify each expression completely.

(a) (2x)^{2}(x^{3})^{2}
4x^{2}\times x^{6}
=4x^{2+6}=\boldsymbol{4x^{8}}
(b) \dfrac{(3m^{2})^{2}}{m^{3}}
\dfrac{9m^{4}}{m^{3}}
=9m^{4-3}=\boldsymbol{9m}
(c) \left(\dfrac{2a^{2}}{b}\right)^{3}
\dfrac{2^{3}\left(a^{2}\right)^{3}}{b^{3}}
=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{8a^{6}}{b^{3}}}
Think In (c), why isn’t the answer \dfrac{2a^{6}}{b^{3}}?
The 2 is inside the brackets, so it is cubed too: 2^{3}=8. Only a number outside the brackets is left alone.
✓

Key ideas

Power of a Product: (ab)^{n}=a^{n}b^{n}. The outer exponent goes to every factor inside, numbers too: (2m)^{4}=16m^{4}.
Power of a Quotient: \left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^{n}=\frac{a^{n}}{b^{n}}, b\neq0. Raise the top and the bottom: \left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{3}=\frac{x^{3}}{8}.
Watch the fence. The exponent repeats only what is inside the brackets: 5m^{3} is not (5m)^{3}=125m^{3}.
Several laws? Distribute the outer exponent first, then combine matching bases. One law per step. Check with a number if you are unsure.
