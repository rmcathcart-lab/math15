Combining Exponent Laws

No new law today. You already have all five. The skill is choosing which one to use first, second and third. A short checklist does the choosing for you: brackets first, then matching bases, then the numbers.

I can…
  • name the five exponent laws and spot which one fits
  • use the checklist: (1) clear the brackets, (2) combine matching bases, (3) coefficients last
  • simplify expressions with two variable bases, one base at a time
  • simplify inside a bracket first when it holds a division, then apply the outer power
AN3 Demonstrate an understanding of powers with integral and rational exponents.
Today: using two or three exponent laws in one expression, in a sensible order.
1

The Laws So Far, and the Order to Use Them

No new law here, just the five you already have. (Tap a card to see its law.)

Product
a^{m}\times a^{n}=a^{m+n}
Quotient
a^{m}\div a^{n}=a^{m-n}
Power of a Power
(a^{m})^{n}=a^{mn}
Power of a Product
(ab)^{n}=a^{n}b^{n}
Power of a Quotient
\left(\dfrac{a}{b}\right)^{n}=\dfrac{a^{n}}{b^{n}}

The checklist: work it the same way every time

1
Clear any bracket with an outer exponent (if the bracket holds a division, simplify inside it first). Raise the coefficient and every variable to that power.
2
Combine matching bases. Add the exponents when multiplying, subtract when dividing.
3
Coefficients last. Multiply or divide the numbers.

(2x^{3})^{2}\times3x^{5}=4x^{6}\times3x^{5}=(4\times3)\,x^{6+5}=12x^{11}

Example 1

Name the step

Simplify (3x^{2})^{3}\times2x^{4}, naming the checklist step you use at each stage.

Step 1, clear the bracket: (3x^{2})^{3}=3^{3}x^{2\times3}=27x^{6}, so the expression is 27x^{6}\times2x^{4}.
Step 2, combine matching bases: x^{6}\times x^{4}=x^{6+4}=x^{10}.
Step 3, coefficients last: 27\times2=54.  Answer: \boldsymbol{54x^{10}}
Think Kai wrote (3x^{2})^{3}=3x^{6}. What did he forget?
To cube the 3. It is inside the brackets, so it gets the exponent too: 3^{3}=27, and (3x^{2})^{3}=27x^{6}.
Example 2

Bracket, then divide

Simplify \dfrac{(3x^{4})^{2}}{x^{3}}. Clear the bracket first, then use the quotient law.

Bracket first: (3x^{4})^{2}=3^{2}x^{8}=9x^{8}.
Then the Quotient Law on the x’s: \dfrac{9x^{8}}{x^{3}}=9x^{8-3}=\boldsymbol{9x^{5}}

Why brackets first? Back to the maze

Write every maze number as a power of 2: 4=2^{2}, \times4=\times2^{2}, \div2=\div2^{1}. Now every tile is an exponent law. The “square it” tile acts like a bracket with an outer exponent: it squares everything that came before it.

Expressions with Two Variable Bases

When two different letters appear, treat each base separately. Clear the bracket by raising every factor inside it to the outer power. Then combine the x’s with the x’s and the y’s with the y’s. The coefficients are handled on their own at the end.

Example 3

Two variable bases

Simplify. Each part uses two or three of the exponent laws.

(a) (2a^{3}b)^{2}\times3a^{2}b^{4}
(2a^{3}b)^{2}=4a^{6}b^{2}
4a^{6}b^{2}\times3a^{2}b^{4}
=(4\times3)a^{6+2}b^{2+4}
=\boldsymbol{12a^{8}b^{6}}
(b) \dfrac{(4x^{2}y^{3})^{2}}{2xy^{2}}
Top: (4x^{2}y^{3})^{2}=16x^{4}y^{6}
\dfrac{16x^{4}y^{6}}{2xy^{2}}=(16\div2)x^{4-1}y^{6-2}
=\boldsymbol{8x^{3}y^{4}}
(c) (x^{2}y^{3})^{3}\times(xy^{2})^{2}
(x^{2}y^{3})^{3}=x^{6}y^{9}
(xy^{2})^{2}=x^{2}y^{4}
x^{6+2}y^{9+4}=\boldsymbol{x^{8}y^{13}}
Think Part (c) has no numbers in front. Which checklist step has nothing to do?
Step 3. Every coefficient is an understood 1, and 1\times1=1, so there is nothing to multiply.

Brackets That Contain a Division

If a bracket has a fraction inside it and a power outside it, simplify inside the bracket first with the quotient law. Then apply the outer power to what is left. Doing the division first keeps the numbers small, and it prevents the most common error: forgetting to raise the coefficient.

Step by step, one law at a time

The same checklist, with one extra move: when a bracket holds a division, do that division first.

Example 4

Brackets, bases, coefficients

Simplify. Brackets first, then matching bases, then the coefficients.

(a) \dfrac{(3x^{2})^{3}\times x^{4}}{9x^{7}}
(3x^{2})^{3}=27x^{6}, so the top is 27x^{6}\times x^{4}=27x^{10}.
\dfrac{27x^{10}}{9x^{7}}=(27\div9)x^{10-7}=\boldsymbol{3x^{3}}
(b) \left(\dfrac{6a^{4}b^{2}}{2a^{2}b}\right)^{2}\times a^{3}
Inside first: \dfrac{6a^{4}b^{2}}{2a^{2}b}=3a^{2}b.
Outer power: (3a^{2}b)^{2}=9a^{4}b^{2}.
Then 9a^{4}b^{2}\times a^{3}=\boldsymbol{9a^{7}b^{2}}
Think In (b), try the outer power first instead. Do you get the same answer?
Yes, but with bigger numbers: \dfrac{36a^{8}b^{4}}{4a^{4}b^{2}}=9a^{4}b^{2}, then \times a^{3}=9a^{7}b^{2}. Inside first keeps the numbers small.
Example 5

Which step does what?

Simplify \left(\dfrac{18x^{6}y^{3}}{6x^{2}y}\right)^{2}\times x^{3}. Hint: which step handles the division inside the bracket, and which handles the power outside it?

The Quotient Law handles the inside; the Power of a Product (and Power of a Power) Law handles the outside.
Inside: \dfrac{18x^{6}y^{3}}{6x^{2}y}=3x^{4}y^{2}.  Outside: (3x^{4}y^{2})^{2}=9x^{8}y^{4}.
Finally 9x^{8}y^{4}\times x^{3}=\boldsymbol{9x^{11}y^{4}}

The big one. Zig-zag A and Zig-zag B also use the same three tiles (×4, square it, ÷2), just in reverse order. How do their finishes compare? Guess first.
A exactly the same

✓

Key ideas

Five laws, no new ones. Same base ×: add. Same base ÷: subtract. Power of a power: multiply. A power of a product or quotient goes to every factor.
The checklist: (1) clear the brackets, raising the coefficient and every variable; (2) combine matching bases; (3) coefficients last.
Two letters? One base at a time: the x’s with the x’s, the y’s with the y’s, the numbers on their own.
A division inside a bracket? Simplify inside first, then apply the outer power. Smaller numbers, fewer slips.
