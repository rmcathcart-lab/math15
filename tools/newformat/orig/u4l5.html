Factoring the Difference of Squares

Multiply a sum by a difference and the middle terms cancel: (a+b)(a-b)=a^2-b^2. Today we run that shortcut backwards to factor, and learn to spot when it does not apply.

I can…
  • factor a^2-b^2 as (a+b)(a-b): take the square root of each term, then write one bracket with + and one with −
  • spot perfect squares quickly: square numbers, and letters with even exponents
  • take out the GCF first, then factor completely
  • tell when a binomial is not a difference of squares, and check my answer by expanding
AN5 Demonstrate an understanding of common factors and trinomial factoring, concretely, pictorially and symbolically.

1

Reversing the Pattern

Multiplying a sum by a difference cancels the middle terms: (a+b)(a-b)=a^2-b^2. Read that backwards and you have a factoring rule.

Difference of squares
a^2-b^2=(a+b)(a-b)
factor →← expand
How to factor it: take the square root of each term, then write one bracket with a + and one with a −.
x^2-64=x^2-8^2=(x+8)(x-8)

Why it works: cut and move

Take a square, a by a, and cut a smaller square, b by b, out of its corner. The L-shape left over has area a^2-b^2. Cut it into two pieces and slide one over: it becomes a rectangle a+b long and a-b tall.

!
One plus, one minus. x^2-36\neq(x-6)^2. Expanding (x-6)(x-6) gives x^2-12x+36, which has a middle term. The brackets must be (x+6)(x-6) so the middle terms cancel.
Example 1

The bare pattern

Factor each difference of squares.

(a) q^2-1
\sqrt{q^2}=q, \sqrt1=1
=\boldsymbol{(q+1)(q-1)}
(b) m^2-16
\sqrt{m^2}=m, \sqrt{16}=4
=\boldsymbol{(m+4)(m-4)}
(c) c^2-100
\sqrt{c^2}=c, \sqrt{100}=10
=\boldsymbol{(c+10)(c-10)}
(d) x^2-y^2
\sqrt{x^2}=x, \sqrt{y^2}=y
=\boldsymbol{(x+y)(x-y)}
Think Back to the warm-up: x^2-9=(x+3)(x-3). Put x=10 into both sides. What do you get?
10^2-9=\boldsymbol{91} and (10+3)(10-3)=13\times7=\boldsymbol{91}. The same number: that’s Suspect C! Trying a number in both forms is a quick check.
2

Identifying Perfect Squares Quickly

Know the small squares on sight. (Tap a square to see its root.)

Perfect squares to know
A letter power is a perfect square when its exponent is even: x^4=(x^2)^2, x^6=(x^3)^2. A monomial is a perfect square when its number is a square and every exponent is even.
\sqrt{4x^2}=2x,   \sqrt{16x^4}=4x^2,   \sqrt{25y^6}=5y^3   (square root the number, halve each exponent)

Numbers in front? Same steps

When a term has a number in front, its square root includes the root of the number: 4x^2-9=(2x)^2-3^2=(2x+3)(2x-3). After factoring, look again: a bracket may be a difference of squares too.

Example 2

Numbers and powers

Factor each difference of squares.

(a) 4x^2-9
\sqrt{4x^2}=2x, \sqrt9=3
=\boldsymbol{(2x+3)(2x-3)}
(b) 9a^2-1
\sqrt{9a^2}=3a, \sqrt1=1
=\boldsymbol{(3a+1)(3a-1)}
(c) x^4-16
\sqrt{x^4}=x^2, \sqrt{16}=4
=(x^2+4)(x^2-4)
Keep going: x^2-4=(x+2)(x-2)
=\boldsymbol{(x^2+4)(x+2)(x-2)}
(d) 25p^2-4q^2
\sqrt{25p^2}=5p, \sqrt{4q^2}=2q
=\boldsymbol{(5p+2q)(5p-2q)}
Think In (c), why does x^2+4 stay as it is?
It is a sum of squares, not a difference. The pattern needs a minus (Section 4), so x^2+4 can’t be factored.
3

Remove the GCF First

Always check for a greatest common factor before anything else. A binomial may not look like a difference of squares until the GCF is out: neither 2x^2 nor 18 is a perfect square, but

2x^2-18=2(x^2-9)=2(x+3)(x-3)

Factor completely

Keep going until nothing left inside the brackets can be factored. Four steps, every time:

1GCF out front
2bracket a difference of squares?
3check every new bracket
4check by expanding
!
Right product, not finished. 4x^2-36=(2x+6)(2x-6) multiplies back correctly, but each bracket still has a common factor of 2. Take out the GCF 4 first: 4(x^2-9)=4(x+3)(x-3).
Example 3

A number GCF

Factor completely by first removing the greatest common factor.

(a) 2x^2-18
GCF 2:  2(x^2-9)
=\boldsymbol{2(x+3)(x-3)}
(b) 3m^2-48
GCF 3:  3(m^2-16)
=\boldsymbol{3(m+4)(m-4)}
Example 4

A GCF with letters, and a two-letter square

Factor completely by first removing the greatest common factor.

(a) 5x^3-45x
GCF 5x:  5x(x^2-9)
=\boldsymbol{5x(x+3)(x-3)}
(b) 8a^2b^2-32
GCF 8:  8(a^2b^2-4)
\sqrt{a^2b^2}=ab, so
=\boldsymbol{8(ab+2)(ab-2)}
Think Mia factored 3x^3-12x as 3(x^3-4x) and stopped. What would you tell her?
The GCF is 3x, not 3: both terms still share an x. 3x(x^2-4), and that bracket is a difference of squares: \boldsymbol{3x(x+2)(x-2)}.
4

When It Is NOT a Difference of Squares

The pattern needs both conditions: subtraction, and two perfect squares.
A sum such as x^2+9 cannot be factored over the integers.
x^2-20 can’t be either: 20 is not a perfect square (16 and 25 are its neighbours).

Check all three before you factor

First term a perfect square? Second term a perfect square? A minus between them? Only when all three pass is it a difference of squares. (If one fails, look for a GCF first: it might pass after.)

Example 5

Difference of squares, or not?

Decide whether each is a difference of squares. If it is, factor it; if not, explain why not.

(a) x^2+9
No. It is a sum of squares. Not factorable over the integers.
(b) x^2-9
Yes:
=\boldsymbol{(x+3)(x-3)}
(c) 4y^2-15
No. 4y^2 is a perfect square but 15 is not (3^2=9,\ 4^2=16).
(d) 16x^2-1
Yes: \sqrt{16x^2}=4x
=\boldsymbol{(4x+1)(4x-1)}
Think Change one thing in 4y^2-15 so that it does factor. How many ways can you find?
Make the 15 a square: 4y^2-16=4(y+2)(y-2) (GCF first!), 4y^2-9=(2y+3)(2y-3), 4y^2-25=(2y+5)(2y-5), 4y^2-1=(2y+1)(2y-1)…
✓

Key ideas

a^2-b^2=(a+b)(a-b). Take the square root of each term; one bracket +, one bracket −. Check by expanding: the middle terms cancel.
Perfect squares: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100, … and even exponents. \sqrt{16x^4}=4x^2: root the number, halve the exponent.
GCF first, then factor completely: 5x^3-45x=5x(x+3)(x-3). Look again at every bracket: x^4-16=(x^2+4)(x+2)(x-2).
Not a difference of squares: a sum like x^2+9, or a term that isn’t a perfect square, like x^2-20.
