Factoring Perfect Square Trinomials

Squaring a binomial always gives three terms: (x+5)^2=x^2+10x+25. Today we run that pattern backwards, and learn to tell a true perfect square from an impostor.

I can…
  • use the three-part test to decide whether a trinomial is a perfect square trinomial
  • factor the sum case a^2+2ab+b^2=(a+b)^2 and the difference case a^2-2ab+b^2=(a-b)^2
  • spot an impostor and factor it with the sum–product method instead
  • check every answer by expanding
AN5 Demonstrate an understanding of common factors and trinomial factoring, concretely, pictorially and symbolically.

The Pattern and the Three-Part Test

Squaring a binomial always gives three terms. Name the tiles from the warm-up (big =x^2, strip =x, small =1) and every square you built is one of these:

sum casea^2+2ab+b^2=(a+b)^2
difference casea^2-2ab+b^2=(a-b)^2

Where does the 2 come from? Look at the square from the side: two equal rectangles.

Can the tiles make a square?

A perfect square trinomial is exactly a set of tiles that builds a square: the strips split evenly between two sides, and the small squares fill the corner exactly.

The three-part test

A trinomial is a perfect square trinomial only if it passes all three:
  1. the first term is a perfect square;
  2. the last term is a perfect square (and positive);
  3. the middle term equals 2\times\sqrt{\text{first}}\times\sqrt{\text{last}}.
For t^2+16t+64: roots t and 8, and 2(t)(8)=16t matches. All three pass.

Factoring the Sum Case

If the test passes and the middle term is positive, take the square root of the first term and of the last term, then write their sum, squared. No guess-and-check is needed!

x^2+10x+25:\quad \sqrt{x^2}=x,\ \ \sqrt{25}=5,\ \ 2(x)(5)=10x\ \checkmark\quad\Rightarrow\quad (x+5)^2

Factoring the Difference Case

Compare x^2-10x+25: same first and last terms, same roots x and 5, and 2(x)(5)=10x still matches. But the middle term is negative, so the binomial being squared is a difference: x^2-10x+25=(x-5)^2.

Only the middle sign changes. The last term is always added, since a square is never negative.

Same steps, minus sign in the bracket

!
Don’t mix up the patterns. x^2-10x+25=(x-5)^2 has a middle term. x^2-25=(x+5)(x-5) (Lesson 5) has none. Count the terms first.

Perfect Square, or an Ordinary Trinomial?

Most trinomials are not perfect squares, so run the test first. If it passes, use the shortcut. If it fails, fall back on the sum–product method: two numbers that multiply to c and add to b.

Classic near-misses. x^2+10x+24 looks almost like (x+5)^2=x^2+10x+25, but 24 is not a perfect square: (x+4)(x+6). And Kit D, x^2+10x+16, has the right squares but the wrong middle: 2(x)(4)=8x\neq10x, so (x+2)(x+8).

Think Why can the last term of a perfect square trinomial never be negative?
It is b^2, a number times itself. Positive × positive and negative × negative are both positive: 5^2=25 and (-5)^2=25.

Key ideas

a^2+2ab+b^2=(a+b)^2 and a^2-2ab+b^2=(a-b)^2. The 2 is the two equal rectangles in the square.
Three-part test: first term a square, last term a square (and +), middle =2\times\sqrt{\text{first}}\times\sqrt{\text{last}}.
Factor: square roots in one bracket, joined by the middle sign, then square it: x^2-10x+25=(x-5)^2. Check by expanding.
Impostor? If any test fails, use sum–product: x^2+10x+24=(x+4)(x+6).
