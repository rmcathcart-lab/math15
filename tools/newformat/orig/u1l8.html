Integers & BEDMASLesson 1
Same signs add and keep the sign; different signs subtract and keep the sign of the larger. Subtracting is adding the opposite. Like signs multiply/divide to +, unlike to -. Order: brackets, exponents, \times\div left to right, +- left to right. Brackets matter: -n^{2}\neq(-n)^{2}.
-5-(-8)=3
(-6)(4)=-24
-4^{2}=-16 but (-4)^{2}=16
Fractions & decimalsLesson 2
Adding or subtracting needs a common denominator; multiplying does not (cancel common factors first); dividing means multiply by the reciprocal. Fraction to decimal: divide. Terminating decimal to fraction: place value, then reduce.
\tfrac{2}{3}+\tfrac{1}{4}=\tfrac{11}{12}
\tfrac{3}{4}\div\tfrac{1}{2}=\tfrac{3}{4}\times\tfrac{2}{1}=\tfrac{3}{2}
0.65=\tfrac{65}{100}=\tfrac{13}{20}
Primes, GCF, LCMLesson 3
A factor tree breaks a composite number into one unique product of primes. The GCF multiplies only the primes in both numbers, at the lowest shared power; the LCM multiplies every prime in either number, at the highest power present.
84=2^{2}\times3\times7
GCF(12,18)=2\times3=6
LCM(12,18)=2^{2}\times3^{2}=36
The number setsLesson 4
The sets nest: N\subset W\subset I\subset Q\subset R, and \overline{Q} (irrational: a decimal that never ends and never repeats) holds the only reals outside that chain. A root is rational only when the radicand is a perfect power.
7: N,W,I,Q,R
-\sqrt{25}=-5: I,Q,R
\sqrt{30}: \overline{Q},R
RadicalsLessons 5–7
Entire to mixed: pull out the largest perfect nth-power factor (use prime factorization when it is not obvious). Mixed to entire: raise the coefficient to the power n and multiply it back into the radicand. An odd index absorbs a negative; an even index cannot.
\sqrt{75}=\sqrt{25}\cdot\sqrt{3}=5\sqrt{3}
\sqrt[3]{128}=4\sqrt[3]{2}
2\sqrt{11}=\sqrt{4\times11}=\sqrt{44}

Fractions and Decimals

Adding or subtracting needs a common denominator: cut the pieces to the same size first.

Multiplying, dividing, and decimals to fractions

Multiplying needs no common denominator: cancel common factors first. Dividing means multiply by the reciprocal. A terminating decimal becomes a fraction by place value, then reduce.

Example 2

Fractions and decimals

Evaluate, using the correct order of operations. Cancel common factors first in (b).

(a) \dfrac{5}{6}-\dfrac{1}{4}
LCD =12:
=\frac{10}{12}-\frac{3}{12}
=\boldsymbol{\frac{7}{12}}
(b) \dfrac{3}{10}\times\dfrac{4}{9}
Cancel 3 with 9, and 4 with 10:
=\frac{1}{5}\times\frac{2}{3}
=\boldsymbol{\frac{2}{15}}
(c) \dfrac{2}{3}+\dfrac{1}{2}\times\dfrac{1}{4}
Multiply first:
=\frac{2}{3}+\frac{1}{8}

35 hundredths:

Prime Factorization, GCF, and LCM

Split a number into any two factors, and keep splitting until every branch ends in a prime. Every way of splitting ends with the same primes. Tap a number in the tree to split it.

Start with:

GCF and LCM, straight from the primes

Put each number’s primes in its own circle; primes they share go in the overlap. GCF = multiply the overlap. LCM = multiply everything in the picture.

Example 3

Prime factorization, GCF, and LCM

Use prime factorization for each.

(a) Find the prime factorization of 90.
90=2\times45
=2\times3\times15
=2\times3\times3\times5
=\boldsymbol{2\times3^{2}\times5}
(b) Find the GCF of 40 and 60.
40=2^{3}\times5
60=2^{2}\times3\times5
Shared primes, lowest power:
GCF =2^{2}\times5=\boldsymbol{20}
(c) Find the LCM of 9 and 15.
9=3^{2}
15=3\times5
Every prime, highest power:
LCM =3^{2}\times5=\boldsymbol{45}

Perfect Powers and Estimating Roots

A perfect square or cube has a whole-number root: use the reference strip. Any other square root is trapped between two consecutive whole numbers: find the perfect squares just below and just above, then see which one it is nearer to.

Example 5

Perfect powers and estimating roots

(a) Evaluate \sqrt{100} and \sqrt[3]{216} mentally.
10\times10=100, so \sqrt{100}=\boldsymbol{10}.
6\times6\times6=216, so \sqrt[3]{216}=\boldsymbol{6}.
(b) Estimate \sqrt{70}: state the two consecutive whole numbers it lies between, state which one it is closer to, and give a one-decimal estimate.
64<70<81, so \boldsymbol{8}<\sqrt{70}<\boldsymbol{9}.
70-64=6 but 81-70=11, so it is closer to 8.
Try tenths: 8.3^{2}=68.89 and 8.4^{2}=70.56; 70 is nearer 70.56.
Estimate: \sqrt{70}\approx\boldsymbol{8.4}

Simplifying Radicals

The index is the group size: pairs for a square root, groups of three for a cube root, four for a fourth root. Each full group of equal prime factors comes out as one number. Going back in, the coefficient goes in as its nth power.

Examples 6 and 7

Simplifying radicals

Write each radical in the form asked for, in simplest form.

6. (a) Convert \sqrt{80} to a mixed radical.
Largest square factor of 80 is 16:
\sqrt{80}=\sqrt{16}\times\sqrt{5}
=\boldsymbol{4\sqrt{5}}
(b) Use prime factorization to convert \sqrt{147} to a mixed radical.
147=3\times7^{2}
\sqrt{147}=\sqrt{7^{2}}\times\sqrt{3}
=\boldsymbol{7\sqrt{3}}
(c) Convert 6\sqrt{5} to an entire radical.
Square the coefficient: 6^{2}=36.
6\sqrt{5}=\sqrt{36\times5}
=\boldsymbol{\sqrt{180}}
7. (a) Convert \sqrt[3]{135} to a mixed radical.
135=27\times5 and 27 is a perfect cube:
\sqrt[3]{135}=\sqrt[3]{27}\times\sqrt[3]{5}
=\boldsymbol{3\sqrt[3]{5}}
(b) Convert 4\sqrt[3]{3} to an entire radical.
Index 3, so cube the coefficient: 4^{3}=64.
4\sqrt[3]{3}=\sqrt[3]{64\times3}
=\boldsymbol{\sqrt[3]{192}}
(c) Convert \sqrt[4]{162} to a mixed radical.
162=81\times2 and 81=3^{4}:
\sqrt[4]{162}=\sqrt[4]{81}\times\sqrt[4]{2}
=\boldsymbol{3\sqrt[4]{2}}
Challenges 1 and 2

Mixed skills

1. Simplify \sqrt{242} to a mixed radical. Then state whether the simplified value is rational or irrational, and explain how you know.
242=2\times11^{2}, so 121 is the largest square factor:
\sqrt{242}=\sqrt{121}\times\sqrt{2}=\boldsymbol{11\sqrt{2}}
It is irrational. \sqrt2 is left over, and 2 is not a perfect square, so \sqrt{2}=1.41421356\ldots never ends and never repeats. Multiplying by 11 can’t fix that, so 11\sqrt2 can’t be written as a fraction of two integers.
2. A rectangular field measuring 75 m by 90 m is going to be tiled with the largest possible identical square sections, with no leftover strip along either side.
(a) Use the GCF of 75 and 90 to find the side length of the largest possible square section.
75=3\times5^{2}, 90=2\times3^{2}\times5
GCF =3\times5=15, so the squares are 15 m on a side.
Check: 75\div15=5 and 90\div15=6, both whole. ✓
(b) A second square section has an area of 75\text{ m}^{2}. Is its side length rational or irrational? Estimate it to the nearest tenth of a metre.
Side =\sqrt{75}, and 75 is not a perfect square, so it is irrational.
\sqrt{75}=5\sqrt{3}, with 64<75<81, so it is between 8 and 9.
Try tenths: 8.6^{2}=73.96 and 8.7^{2}=75.69; 75 is nearer 75.69.
\sqrt{75}\approx\boldsymbol{8.7} m

Key ideas

Fixed rules, same answer. Signs follow the integer rules, and BEDMAS sets the order. Brackets matter: -4^2=-16 but (-4)^2=16.
Fractions: common denominator to add or subtract; cancel, then multiply; flip and multiply to divide. Fractions and decimals are the same numbers written two ways.
Primes first. Every whole number is one product of primes. GCF = the shared primes; LCM = every prime, highest power.
Sets and roots. N\subset W\subset I\subset Q\subset R, irrationals beside the rationals. A radical simplifies by pulling out its largest perfect square (or cube, …).
